Utkarsh Jain

From Preet Vihar to an International Ranking

"When the universe gives you pressure, it's a privilege and not everyone gets that privilege."

Utkarsh Jain's earliest cricket memories are of playing with his father in the basement of their house. After the family moved to Preet Vihar in East Delhi, he was spending eight or nine hours a day in the local park. His mother saw how much he loved it and enrolled him at Dronacharya Cricket Academy. Nobody in his family had played cricket, so nobody knew how the system worked.

Switching Arms

He started as a right-arm fast bowler. A few years in, he noticed that almost every school cricketer wore a DDCA state badge and almost nobody at his academy did. So he moved his focus to school cricket at DPS Mathura Road and set out to impress coach Randhir Singh.

At home, he had been bowling left-arm spin against a wall. He showed the coaches, and they told him that was his future. "Power naturally resides in your dominant right hand," he says. He kept working at the left arm anyway, and it began to pay off in matches.

His days started at 4:15 AM, with training before school, the JMC academy in the afternoon and tuition at night. He kept that up for three or four years. In ninth grade he turned around the IPSC Under-17 final against Modern School by taking three wickets in one over.

Third in India, and Still Overlooked

At fifteen he played the Vijay Merchant Trophy for Delhi and finished as the third-highest wicket-taker in India. That earned him a place at an Under-19 India camp, but without anyone backing him in the system, his name went no further. It was the first time he realised performance alone wasn't always enough.

In one Under-19 match, the webbing between his fingers split open. He had it stitched immediately, came back, twisted his ankle in the field, and still bowled. The opposition needed thirty runs with eight wickets in hand. He took five wickets and won the game.

Across Borders

When Covid shut everything down, he went to the UK and played minor county cricket in Kent and Essex. The housing he had been promised was taken away, so he found his own place. He finished the season as the leagues' highest wicket-taker and won the Lord's Honours Award.

A scout then invited him to Cambodia, where the national team was being built. He played domestic cricket there while studying remotely with the University of Auckland. He made his international debut in May 2023. With Cambodia he won four golds and two silvers at the Southeast Asian Games, played at the Asian Games in China, and reached 292 in the ICC T20 bowling rankings.

Writing It Down

His early days with Cambodia were hard: a new country, poor form, and pressure from management. He took two days off, sat down with a sheet of paper and wrote out every achievement of his career. He went into the next match playing for the love of the game, took a sharp catch and two wickets, and conceded very few runs.

He is back in Delhi now, working through knee rehab and mentoring younger players. His advice to them is the one he wishes he'd had earlier: do sport-specific conditioning, and don't just follow the crowd.

"In highs or lows, one thing you got to do is do your work. Nothing is permanent."
