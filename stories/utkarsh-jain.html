Utkarsh Jain, From a Preet Vihar Park to Playing for Cambodia

"I have always believed that when the universe gives you pressure, it is a privilege."

Utkarsh Jain's fondest early memories are of playing cricket with his father in the basement of their house. When the family moved to Preet Vihar in East Delhi, he found a group of people playing in the local park and joined in to pass the time. He is competitive by nature, and before long he was spending eight or nine hours a day there. His mother saw how much he loved it and enrolled him at the Dronacharya Cricket Academy near their home, where he started as a right arm fast bowler.

Nobody in his family had played cricket, so nobody knew how the system worked. In seventh grade his mother gave him an ultimatum: one year to show results, or he would have to stop. He used that pressure to buy himself more time.

Learning left arm spin

After four or five years of being told he was good, he realised that the friendly matches he played at the academy were not doing much for his career. At DPS Mathura Road, where he studied, he noticed that almost everyone in the school team wore the DDCA state badge, while only one or two players at his academy had it. He left the academy and put his energy into school cricket, with one aim: to catch the eye of the coach, Randhir Singh, a tall, imposing man who did not say much.

At home he had been experimenting, bowling left arm against the wall of his house and then in the park. An older boy in his society saw him and asked whether he could bowl it in a match. Before a tournament, Utkarsh told his coach he could bowl left arm spin as well. The coach showed it to Randhir Singh, who said it was the better option for him. Utkarsh was not sure at first, because, as he puts it, "power naturally resides in your dominant right hand," but he kept working at it, took wickets in that tournament and was encouraged to continue.

In ninth grade he went to Agra with his father for the CBSE Nationals trials, bowled well and was selected. The same year his school reached the final of the IPSC Under 17 tournament against Modern School, Barakhamba Road. The senior players were struggling and the match was slipping away, so he asked the coach for the ball. He took three wickets in one over and another in the next, and turned the game. It was the first time he understood how much he enjoyed bowling under pressure.

His days started at 4:15 in the morning, because there were no cabs from Noida to his school that early. He trained from five to half past six, went to school, and at two in the afternoon went to the academy at JMC College for four or five hours. In the evening he met his tuition teacher, then slept. He kept up that routine for three or four years and kept his grades up as well.

Third in India, and then nothing

At fifteen he played the Under 16 Vijay Merchant Trophy for Delhi and finished as the third highest wicket taker in India. That earned him a place at an Under 19 India camp. But he had nobody in the system pushing his name, and it went no further. It was the first time he realised that performance on its own was not always enough. He later played once for Manipur as a guest player at Under 16 level, returned to Delhi when the guest player rules changed, and made the Delhi Under 19 team in his final year of eligibility.

In one important match the ball split the webbing between his fingers open. At the hospital the doctor told him it would need seven days to heal properly. Utkarsh asked him to stitch it straight away so he could go back. He fielded at third man with his hand bandaged and twisted his leg while running. The opposition needed only 30 runs with eight wickets in hand. He went on to bowl, took five wickets and won the game.

"I have always believed that when the universe gives you pressure, it is a privilege," he says. Just as he was finding momentum again, Covid shut everything down.

England, then Cambodia

He took an opportunity to play minor county cricket in England, in Kent and Essex. Soon after he arrived, there was a contract clash and the housing he had been given was taken away. He found his own place and kept playing in local matches for three or four weeks until a club offered to pay him and cover his accommodation. He finished the season as the highest wicket taker in the Kent and Essex leagues and won the Lord's Honours Award.

He had offers to stay, but he felt the route to playing for England was closed to him, and he set his sights on New Zealand. Around this time he tore the meniscus and cruciate ligament in his knee, and kept playing through it without proper rehab so he would not miss any chances. Then a scout invited him to Cambodia, where the national team was being developed as an associate nation. With New Zealand's borders still closed, he moved there in late 2022, playing domestic cricket and studying for his degree remotely with the University of Auckland.

He made his international debut in May 2023. The first phase was hard: a new country, form that was below his standard, and criticism from the management that left him feeling cornered. He took two days away, sat down with a sheet of paper and listed every achievement of his cricket career. He decided to stop playing to please anyone and to play for the love of the game. In the next match, under heavy pressure, he took a sharp catch and bowled two tight overs for two wickets.

With Cambodia he went on to win four gold medals and two silver medals at the Southeast Asian Games and played at the Asian Games in China. Within a year he was the highest wicket taker in Southeast Asia and had an equipment sponsorship from BDM. He reached 292 in the ICC T20 bowling rankings, which he calls the highest point of his career so far.

Back in Delhi

International politics and the wear in his knee eventually brought him home. He has spent the last year in Delhi on knee rehabilitation and training, and he mentors younger players. The advice he gives them is the advice he would give his younger self. He used to think six hours of cricket a day meant he was fit, and he now believes the lack of sport specific conditioning led to his knee injury. "Don't just follow the crowd, think differently," is how he would put it to his younger self.

He is still training and still open to whatever opportunity comes next. "Whenever I felt low, people around me reminded me of my capability," he says. "Furthermore, cricket is the only thing I truly know and excel at."
