Renee Rane and the Sport That Grew on Her

"It's layered, honestly. Part of it is financial, part of it is that I'm genuinely good at it, part of it is that I've invested too much time to walk away now. But underneath all of that, I do love it."

Renee Rane was born in Hyderabad to army parents, and the family moved often before settling in Delhi for several years. Her father had her try a few sports. Tennis did not stick because, as she puts it, she is the kind of person who needs to be good at something to enjoy it. Around 2012, after the move to Delhi, he took her to a golf course. She was twelve. She does not remember liking it, and at first she was bored. Once she started getting good, she began to enjoy it, and she kept going.

Her father was closely involved in those early years, so closely that it caused real friction between them. She wanted to do things on her own terms, and there was a period when she hated the sport. She would get upset when he sighed on the course. That has changed a great deal. He is supportive now without being overbearing, and he loves watching her play. She still names him as the biggest influence on her golf, since he was the one who first took her to a course.

Committing early

Because she started later than many competitive golfers, Renee understood early that staying in the sport meant committing to it fully. It took her about a year to realise she was good, which she says is quicker than usual for a junior. She also learned how unforgiving the game can be. "Golf punishes inconsistency badly, even a couple of days off can cost you nearly a week of form."

Her breakthrough came on a three city Indian Golf Union circuit that ran from Hyderabad to Bangalore to Mumbai. She reached the Mumbai leg exhausted and not especially focused on winning, but after two days she was in contention, and for the first time she felt what it meant to be near the top of a leaderboard. She won the tournament outright. It was her first national title, and she was sixteen or seventeen and still in high school.

College in New Jersey

Golf took her to the United States, where she was recruited into a Division I college programme in New Jersey. She says the sport is the reason she went to college at all. She expected college to be a continuation of what she already knew, and almost everything turned out to be different. In India her preparation had been narrow: practise, play, repeat. In the US she began building real game plans before tournaments and thinking about how she wanted to carry herself on the course. For the first time she paid attention to nutrition and recovery, and she worked with a sports psychologist and a nutritionist. "It changed me not just as a golfer but as a person."

The game itself was organised differently. In India she had made her own decisions. In American college golf the team comes first, so she follows the coach's direction even when it does not always suit her individual game. Balancing that with schoolwork and looking after herself in a new country was hard. Campus dining was a particular struggle in her first year, when she was required to eat on the meal plan, and afterwards she went to a nutritionist to learn the basics, such as her protein requirements and the habit of prioritising carbohydrates during a round.

Asked whether she ever wanted to quit, she answers, "Constantly, actually, during my first year of college." She was homesick almost all the time and found it hard to enjoy her new surroundings. Things turned when she grew close to the swimming and diving team and moved in with a group of girls from it. Having her own space, away from running into the same faces every day, made a real difference.

Managing the mind

Renee used to overthink, fixating on scores and numbers instead of staying in the present. Anxiety is her main mental struggle. Her sports psychologist taught her a five, four, three grounding exercise, in which she notices things around her through her senses, and she can use it in the middle of a round. Team commitments sometimes mean she has to play when she does not feel ready. That used to affect her a lot, though it has eased as her swing has become more settled. She has cried on the course before. When it happens, she takes a few seconds to reset and carries on.

Unlike many athletes, she prefers to be around people before a round, because being alone makes her more stressed, not less. She does not meditate, although many people around her do. Her approach to setbacks is simple: go in, try her best and consciously let go of whatever does not work out.

Her hardest stretch came last year, when she was changing her swing while dealing with physical and health complications. She describes it as not dramatic, but difficult to move through. She ended up changing nearly everything about her approach on outside advice, and it worked well. Her previous coach had been excellent and, in many ways, more of a life coach. She switched to someone far more technical, and she now has to think much less about mechanics while she plays.

Where she is now

Golf has changed her socially too. She was a shy child, and hours spent paired with strangers during rounds taught her how to hold a conversation and feel comfortable with people. Away from the course she describes herself as fairly funny, listens to all kinds of music, swims, reads almost everything, and plays tennis, badminton and chess without claiming to be good at any of them. Even so, she admits she struggles to picture herself without golf.

She is honest about why she continues. "It's layered, honestly. Part of it is financial, part of it is that I'm genuinely good at it, part of it is that I've invested too much time to walk away now. But underneath all of that, I do love it."

If she had unlimited resources, she would put them into women's golf in India, with better maintained courses and more access for women. She points out that women are underrepresented across most sports, and golf is no exception.

Renee is double majoring in Finance and Technology, and Marketing. She is open to turning professional but has not decided. She loves living in America and would want a stable path there, which means weighing visas and the real cost of going pro, and she is not yet sure she has the confidence to commit to it. If she does, she expects to start on the Indian circuit, the route most players she knows have taken. For now there is a tournament coming up to prepare for. Asked what she would tell her younger self, she says: "That it's worth it. That it leads to college, to an education, and that it all comes together if you stay with it."
