Ammar Abidi

Rejected Twice, Never Gave Up

"I would tell myself that I'm not alone."

Ammar Abidi's parents put him in a cricket academy first. Around fourth grade he watched his cousins play football and changed sports. His parents were glad he had found a sport he chose himself.

Two Years Outside the Team

In fourth and fifth grade, he didn't make his school football team, even though nearly all his closest friends did. "I acted cool in front of my friends and everyone. But obviously inside I was, I had broken every year for those two years."

In sixth grade, coaches suggested he try hockey or basketball, where fewer kids were competing. He tried out for football again and got in. His father's constant encouragement kept him going.

Gurgaon to Nationals

By seventh grade he could see that his discipline set him apart. He joined Fast Football Club in Gurgaon under Deepak Sir, who let him train with players aged 18 and 19 and made the academy feel like home. That year he was picked for the Gurgaon district team and played at state level, and he kept his place for two or three years. In eleventh grade he represented Haryana at the Nationals, and they finished runners-up to West Bengal.

Between eighth and eleventh grade, though, there were trials where he scored four or five goals in a match and still wasn't picked because the lists had already been decided. It was the hardest phase of his career, and it hit his motivation hard.

The Hamstring

After Nationals he pulled his hamstring. With no one to advise him, he rested for a week and went back to playing, which made it much worse. He was out for eight to ten months and put on weight because he was still eating as if he were training. He spent most of twelfth grade getting fit again. Even so, he captained Gurgaon to second place in Haryana that year.

SRCC and Beyond

He got into SRCC, his dream college, through the sports quota. In his first year he was the team's second-highest scorer. In his second year he came into the inter-college tournament unfit and was called out for it, which hit him hard because he felt he owed the college his best.

He is now the college's sports secretary and helps run an annual event for around 15,000 student athletes. He wants to play in the Delhi Premier League and win inter-college in his final year. Early training, he says, makes the rest of his day more productive. "Without this sport, I feel a little empty."

If he could talk to his younger self, he would say that the setbacks were normal: "I'm not alone. Everyone is going through this."
