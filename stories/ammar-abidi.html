Ammar Abidi and the School Team That Turned Him Down Twice

"I acted cool in front of my friends and everyone. But obviously inside I was, I had broken every year for those two years."

Ammar Abidi failed to make his school football team in Class 4, and again in Class 5. Almost all the friends he thought of as his best friends were in it. "I acted cool in front of my friends and everyone," he says. "But obviously inside I was, I had broken every year for those two years."

Football had not been his first sport. His parents wanted him to play something and put him in a cricket academy. Around Class 4 he watched his cousins playing football and found it more interesting. His parents were happy about the switch, because this time the interest was his own. He still plays cricket with friends for fun.

What kept him going through the two rejections, he says, was his father, who stayed positive whatever happened. In Class 6 the coaches suggested he try out for hockey or basketball instead, where fewer boys were competing for places. Ammar tried out for football again and was selected.

Gurgaon, Haryana and the nationals

By Class 7 he had noticed that he was more disciplined than most of his teammates, and that this set him apart. He joined Fast Football Club, where the coach, Deepak Sir, trusted his players and let Ammar train and play with boys of 18 and 19 while he was still young. There were no strict batch timings. Players could come in the morning or the evening, train alone, or come in pairs at weekends. He says the academy felt like home.

That same year, at about 12, he was picked for the Gurgaon district team and played at state level. He kept his place for two or three years and began playing league football for clubs. In Class 11 he represented Haryana at the nationals, where the team reached the final and lost to West Bengal.

The years between were harder than the results suggest. He remembers going to trials in different districts of Haryana, scoring four or five goals in a match and still not being picked, and being told that the selections had already been decided. At that age football was all he thought about, and he wanted to play professionally. He believed in himself, and he calls this the toughest phase of his career. He does not think it hurt his performances directly, but it wore down his motivation.

The hamstring

After the nationals he pulled a hamstring. It was the first injury of his career, and he had never missed a game through injury before. With no one to guide him, he rested for a week, felt no pain, and went back to playing. The injury got much worse, and he was out for eight to ten months.

Because he was still eating as he had when he was training, he put on weight quickly, and that became the bigger problem. He spent most of Class 12 getting fit again. He says a little more guidance and encouragement at the time would have helped, because he felt very demotivated watching his weight go up and his game go down.

On paper Class 12 went well. He captained the Gurgaon district team, which finished second in Haryana, and at the end of the year he won a place at SRCC, his dream college, through the sports quota. But he was not happy with his own form, and he describes that year as the time he felt most drained.

College football

In his first year at SRCC he was the team's second highest scorer, and they went deep into the inter college tournament. In his second year he came into the same tournament unfit. He played poorly in the first match and in the second, which they lost, and the head of department told him he was very unfit, and that as a sports quota student he should be representing the college at his best. It hit him hard because he agreed. He felt he owed the college good performances, and football had always been the part of his life where he expected to do well.

Keeping fit is still his main struggle. During exams and internships he cannot play as much and the weight comes back. When he is training regularly, though, he finds that getting up early for it makes the rest of the day better. "It's when I'm more sharp or energized throughout the day, that's when I feel my classes are more productive," he says.

He is now the college's sports secretary and helps run its sports events, including an annual event that brings in around 15,000 student athletes from across Delhi. He likes leading things and credits football for that.

What he wants now

Ammar no longer plans to play professionally. He wants to keep playing for clubs in the Delhi leagues and in university competitions, and to carry on after college wherever work takes him. "Without this sport, I feel a little empty," he says. His short term goals are to get fitter, to play for a club in the Delhi Premier League, and to win the inter college tournament with SRCC in his final year.

Looking back, he wishes he had stepped out of his comfort zone and trialled with other clubs and in other states, as his father kept urging him to. To his younger self, facing the selection trials and the injury, he would say that these things are normal. "I'm not alone. Everyone is going through this thing." He describes himself as an athlete with a never give up attitude, disciplined, and humble, which he says is what he wants to be.
