Aditya Malhotra

Aditya Malhotra and the first ball he faced in the DPL

"Now that I think about it, I feel like I started working harder after that."

Aditya Malhotra started playing cricket at four or five, right after the 2011 World Cup, when the game was everywhere in India. It began as an after school hobby. Back then, he says, nobody around him really knew the process for reaching the India team. You played at an academy, you got good, and you assumed a chance would come one day.

In school cricket he was one of the better players, and by six or seven he had started to think this was something he actually wanted to do. Even so, he believes the real journey begins later. "I feel like a cricketer journey actually starts when he is 12 or 13," he says, because that is when players start giving trials and learn what state cricket means.

Academies and trials

He moved between academies often, usually on the advice of seniors, and has trained under six or seven coaches. He credits all of them. For the last two years he has been with Mr Ajit and Mr Sanjay. Ajit has pushed him hard, giving him chances in senior tournaments where Aditya himself did not expect to play.

He was picked for Delhi's Under 16 team in the 2022 to 23 season, but the tournament was cancelled because of the third Covid wave. Later, in the 50 over trial matches, he scored a century in each of his two games in the first round and then two unbeaten fifties in the final round. In another trial game, after he got out early with the bat, his coach told him to try bowling, and he took five wickets. As a registered player he was going to the top 60 camp anyway, but every performance helped.

In August 2025 he was picked for the Delhi Premier League, which he calls the first big achievement of his career. In October he made the top 20 of Delhi's Under 19 squad for the Vinoo Mankad Trophy, the 50 over competition between the states.

One ball on live television

The DPL did not go well for him. By the time he got a game, his team was already out of the tournament. He walked out on live television to face Simarjeet Singh, a fast bowler with IPL experience, and went to the crease as quickly as he could. The first ball was a wide. Then the bowler spent two or three minutes fixing his run up. "I felt like it was like 10, 15, 20 minutes." That was when it sank in that thousands of people were watching. The next ball was a yorker at around 140 kmph, and he was out.

Getting out first ball on live television, after waiting so long for the chance, was hard to take, and he says nobody could have said anything to him in that moment. "Now that I think about it, I feel like I started working harder after that." He still counts the DPL as a good learning experience, because it meant training with older, more experienced players. He admits he was quiet and a little underconfident at the start of the camp, before the squad had settled in together. He knew four or five of his teammates personally and had played with most of the others somewhere in Delhi's club and academy cricket.

Time away

The Under 19 season was harder. He was in the squad and among the highest run getters, but he never got a game for Delhi. He felt disconnected from a dream he had been chasing for years, so he took two or three weeks off and travelled. "If I played during that time, I just would have started hating this game." He does not wish failure on the players picked ahead of him. "If someone else is getting the chance, I want what's best to them," he says. "I'm not that kind of person."

He has been spared serious injuries. About a year ago a lower back strain from a run of matches kept him out for three or four weeks. He used to bowl fast, but his father had seen how often fast bowlers break down with knee and hamstring injuries, and he has a weak spine himself, so he told Aditya to stop. He did, at nine or ten. He is now a batting all rounder who bats in the middle order but prefers to open. The powerplay, facing the new ball, is his favourite part of the game. "Even when I watch cricket, that is the phase that I watch with full intention."

He credits his parents and his sister for supporting him through the ups and downs. He grew up watching Virat Kohli, remembering the Test matches in particular, and these days he admires Ishan Kishan, both for his batting and for the way he fought his way back into the India side.

What comes next

He turns 19 in August and moves up to Under 23 trials, where he knows players are rarely considered in their first year or two. His plan for the next two years is to do well in the DPL and earn calls to IPL trials. When he started, the IPL was not so big and everyone's goal was simply India; now he sees strong DPL performances leading to IPL contracts and thinks of that as a shorter route. The long term goal has not changed since he was a small boy watching the World Cup: to play for India.
