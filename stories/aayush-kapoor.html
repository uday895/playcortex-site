Aayush Kapoor

Aayush Kapoor, the skating champion who started again in badminton

"I went from a very good patch to just lying on my bed."

Aayush Kapoor was a national champion before he played a serious game of badminton. He started skating at four, racing on quads as a road skater, first at the Chilla Sports Complex and later in Noida. He won the Delhi state championship sixteen times and the national championship three years running, in 2013, 2014 and 2015. Along the way he collected four CBSE golds and two SGFI golds.

2015 was his last season. He won the national championships that year, and he also broke both of the supporting bones at his elbow. Doctors told him that another fall on his left hand would make it useless. Skating offered little future in India anyway, he says, because it is not part of the Olympics or the Commonwealth Games. He left the sport.

Starting again

Badminton was already in the family. His grandfather had been an international badminton player, and his father played casually. Aayush used to watch his father, came to like the game, and his father wanted him to make a career of it. On 3 January 2017 he began playing professionally, under a coach who treated him as a professional player and stayed with him for about four years.

The national champion was a beginner again. He spent a year and a half on the basics and played local grassroots tournaments for experience. His first official tournament was the 2019 District Championships in Mayur Vihar, where, at about twelve, he won the Under 13 singles and finished second in doubles with his brother. State championships followed, and in 2020 he played his first All India tournament, in Guwahati, just before the lockdown.

He kept going when the courts closed. He put up a net in his housing society and played outdoors, and a fitness trainer who lived in the society, himself a badminton player, trained them from six to eight every morning and every evening. "I wanted to stay connected with the sport."

Into the top 15

His first tournament after the lockdown, in July 2021, was the Delhi Badminton League, a team event with a prize pool of 28 lakh rupees. His team finished as runners up and he took home ₹48,000. He calls it a major breakthrough, because it gave him confidence and put him alongside senior players. He then qualified for the main draw of an All India tournament in Goa, and in 2022 he reached a quarterfinal that lifted his national ranking into the top 15. In badminton, he points out, selection depends on your ranking across the national circuit rather than on trials alone.

International events followed. In August 2023 he played the India Junior International Series at the Pullela Gopichand Academy in Hyderabad, and he also qualified for the Grand Prix there. He has played tournaments in Thailand and Indonesia, and had the chance to play junior events on the European circuit, such as the Dutch Open and the Irish Open.

College and a lean patch

He joined SRCC in 2023 through the sports trials, where he reached the semifinals, and is studying for a B.Com (Honours). His coaching starts at four in the morning; he leaves at 7:30 and goes straight to college. Attendance is a constant struggle, and he feels the college limits how much he can travel: "if I had more time, I would have gone to more places." Some teachers and students treat sports quota students differently, he says, though most people respect that he is managing both.

From 2023 to 2025 he went through what he calls a lean patch. He kept losing close matches, "not because the opponent was better, but because I lacked confidence." He was carrying a fear he could not shake and had started doubting himself, all the more because his father was spending so much money on his trips to nationals. His advice to anyone under that kind of pressure is to step away for five to ten days and play something recreational, such as cricket, until they start loving their sport again.

Back in rehab

Then came the injury. He has a disc bulge at the L4 and L5 vertebrae and scoliosis, and the pressed nerve sends pain down his right leg. The problem began in his skating days, and badminton made it worse. His last tournament before rehab was the Khelo India University Games, where he represented Delhi University. When he was interviewed, he had been in rehabilitation for two and a half months.

"I went from a very good patch to just lying on my bed," he says. "The only question in my mind is if I will be able to play at the same level again." He is trying to take it positively, and the time away has let him spend more of it with his family and his grandparents.

The people he names first are his father and his grandfather, then his first coach, with whom he is still in touch, and two or three friends who have been with him since he was eleven. He plans to take a fourth year at college so he can have one more year of professional badminton before placements. Asked to describe himself, he said he was hardworking, that his journey had been a roller coaster, and that it had been a very eventful life.
