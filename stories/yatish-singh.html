Yatish Singh and the decision to learn leg spin

"It's building like brick by brick."

About a year ago, Yatish Singh made the biggest decision of his cricket life. He had been a pure off spinner, and a successful one, through his Under 19 years and his early Under 23 seasons for Delhi. But T20 cricket had become more explosive, the Delhi Premier League had arrived, and he could see that off spin was not getting him chances in white ball cricket. "Any batsman can maul off spin," he says. So he began learning leg spin and other variations, and stepped away from red ball cricket for a while to do it.

He is 22 and plays for Central Delhi Kings in the DPL. He has played for 12 years and does not believe in sudden breakthroughs. "It's building like brick by brick," he says. "Every year, every session I'm just trying to improve."

Finding a process

He started at around 10 or 12 at Frank Anthony School, joined the school team, and for three or four years played purely for fun. Around 16 he realised that some cricketers are gifted and others have to get there through hard work and a clear process. He had no plan and no path. He would go to practice, bowl, bat and come home, and he was not moving forward. Under 16 trials showed him how far ahead his peers were.

So he left group academy training and started working with personal coaches. He bowled alone in the nets and studied his own biomechanics, technique and emotions with the help of his coaches, trainer and physios. For about two years he worked hard and followed the process. By his second year of Under 19 cricket, he found he was further ahead than he had expected. Teams began calling him for the big Delhi tournaments at around 17 or 18. Alongside cricket he completed a BA in English Honours at Delhi University.

Parents and friends

He was never a student who failed, though he admits he studied only a week or two before exams and got average marks. His parents still worried, because cricket is an uncertain career, and until about ninth grade they kept reminding him that studies mattered. "Parents have to see something to believe in you," he says. Once they saw him practising for hours and playing match after match, they came round, and since his Under 19 days they have backed him completely.

His friendships have been just as steady. He has four or five close friends who have been with him for 10 to 15 years. They hardly ask about cricket and do not judge him for anything. If a player feels judged by his friends for his profession, he says, he is probably in the wrong friend group.

He has never thought about quitting. His family has a business, and for the past two or three years he has been financially independent through his earnings from state cricket. He knows that is not true for everyone, and he has huge respect for players without those resources, who have to choose between the game and supporting their families.

Pressure is a privilege

He likes a line he read recently: pressure is a privilege. He does not pretend the nerves go away. "Nerves are going to be high and the heartbeat is going to be on the rooftop," he says. He takes deep breaths and reminds himself of the work he put in during the off season. "If the preparation is right, I guess pressure can't take you over." He also tells himself that even if the worst happens, he will still go home and sleep in his own room, and people will have forgotten in three or four days.

That, he says, is what sport has given him. In cricket you fail far more often than you succeed, and learning to live with that has made difficult moments in his personal life feel manageable. "Sport can teach you how to deal with your emotions."

Workload and sleep

He only started taking his fitness seriously a couple of years ago, losing fat and building muscle. He has never hired a nutritionist. He likes to read, so he did his own research, watching leading nutritionists on YouTube, with guidance from a trainer. He keeps it simple: enough protein, limited carbohydrates, no sugar, careful timing around practice and an occasional cheat meal as a reward for a hard week.

A qualified strength and conditioning coach manages his training five times a week, with two or three athletics sessions and two or three in the gym, and plans everything around his bowling load. In the off season he bowls a great deal, often practising in the morning and again in the evening around a gym session, building strength and power through volume. As the season approaches, the volume drops and the intensity rises. If he bowls 10 overs, the coach plans his gym or running work around that; after 20 overs the evening is for mobility, stretching and a good meal. He sleeps eight to nine hours a night, and uses ice baths in the heavier weeks.

Something new

Asked what he would tell his younger self, he says he would find a professional coach who matches his vision rather than imposing his own, and start personal coaching early. Given unlimited money, he would first spend some of it on underprivileged children who cannot afford to play cricket, then buy good cars and travel the world.

His plans for his own cricket he keeps mostly to himself. He is working on something in his bowling that he believes no one has done before, and expects it to show in a year or two. "If I say it now, I don't think it's good," he says. "I'll just do it in a couple of years."
