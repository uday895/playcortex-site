Yatish Singh

Brick by brick, spin by spin

"It's building like brick by brick. Every year every session I'm just trying to improve improve and improve."

Yatish Singh does not believe in overnight stories. The 22-year-old Delhi cricketer, who plays for the Central Delhi Kings in the Delhi Premier League, has spent 12 years building his game one session at a time. He has never had a single magical turning point, and he is proud of that. What he has instead is a process, and the clarity to change it when the game demands.

From school team to a clean process

Yatish started playing at around 10 or 12, like any child, joining his school team at Frank Anthony School. For three or four years he simply played for fun. Then, at around 16, he had a realisation that shaped everything after it. Some cricketers, he says, are gifted. Others have to work hard and follow a clean process to reach a certain level. He was just going to practice, bowling and batting and coming home, without a path. It was not moving him forward.

So he stepped away from group academy training and began working with personal coaches. He bowled alone in the nets and studied his own biomechanics, his technique and even his emotions, with help from his coaches, trainer and physios. Under-16 trials had shown him that his peers were better. He introspected, trained hard for about two years, and by his second year of under-19 cricket he realised the process had taken him further than he expected. Teams began calling him to the big Delhi tournaments at 17 and 18.

Parents who needed to see it

Yatish was never a student who failed exams, but his parents worried that cricket was an uncertain field and stressed his studies. He understands why. Parents, he says, need to see something to believe in you. When they watched him practise for hours and play match after match, they came round, and since his under-19 days they have been completely supportive. Alongside cricket, he completed a BA in English Honours at Delhi University.

His friendships are just as steady: four or five close friends who have been with him for 10 to 15 years and do not judge him for anything. If your friends judge your profession, he says, you are probably in the wrong friend group.

Learning a new trade at 21

His biggest setback was really a moment of introspection. Yatish was a pure off-spinner, and he did well in his under-19 and early under-23 years. But as T20 cricket became more explosive and the DPL arrived, he realised that off-spin was not getting him chances in white-ball cricket. So he made a very big decision: he began learning leg-spin and other variations, and stepped away from red-ball cricket for a while to do it. A year into that evolution, he is still working on it with the Central Delhi Kings, building for his T20 future.

He has never thought about quitting. He is grateful that his family's business and his own earnings from state cricket mean he has never faced the impossible choice between sport and supporting a family, and he has huge respect for athletes who do.

Pressure is a privilege

Yatish likes a line he read recently: pressure is a privilege. He knows his heartbeat will be on the rooftop in a big game. He controls it with deep breaths and by remembering the work he put in during the off-season. If the preparation is right, he says, pressure cannot take you over. And if the worst happens, it is not the end of the world.

That perspective is what he values most about sport. In cricket, he says, you fail far more often than you succeed, and that teaches you how to deal with the ups and downs of life. He manages his own nutrition through research, keeping it simple with enough protein, limited carbs and no sugar, and timing his meals around practice. A qualified strength and conditioning coach manages his workload, adjusting gym and running sessions based on how many overs he bowls, and he guards 8 to 9 hours of sleep.

Something no one has done

His advice to his younger self is to find a professional coach who matches your vision rather than imposing his own, and to start personal coaching early. With infinite money, he would support underprivileged children who cannot afford to play, then buy good cars and travel the world. His short-term plan he keeps close to his chest. He is working on something different in his bowling, something he believes no one has done, and he expects it to show in a year or two. True to form, he would rather prove it than say it.
