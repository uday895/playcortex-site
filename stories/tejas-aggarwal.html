Tejas Aggarwal

Tejas Aggarwal, the leg spinner who became a coach at 23

"I always feel that a coach is only as good as a player is."

Tejas Aggarwal never played for Mumbai. He went to the age group camps, bowled leg spin to India players in the nets and, at 22, was told plainly that the gap between his game and the top level was too big. Today he is a professional cricket coach at All Art Academy, the academy run by Abhishek Nayar, has worked two seasons with UP Warriors in the Women's Premier League and scouts young players for Kolkata Knight Riders.

From gloves to leg spin

He started late, at 16, in 2011, playing at Azad Maidan "like every Mumbai kid". He began as a wicketkeeper batter, having grown up watching MS Dhoni and Adam Gilchrist, and he liked being the one player in eleven who wore the gloves. In a practice match crowded with keepers, a coach asked whether he could do anything else. Tejas had been a big Shane Warne fan as a child, so he said he bowled leg spin. He had never bowled it. "It wasn't a brilliant over for sure. It was my first over," he says. "But yes, I was able to at least spin the ball naturally."

Right opposite Azad Maidan is the Bombay Gymkhana, with its long history and green outfield, where every Maidan kid wants to practise. One morning he was left out of a practice match and ended up in tears. That afternoon his side lost, and while he waited for evening practice he noticed crowds of children going into the Gymkhana. They were there for the annual selection trials. He joined in, was called back over the following days, and by the end of the week had earned a place to practise there free for the whole season. "The first half of the day I was in tears and very sad and the second half got selected," he says.

He went on to practise at the CCI, the Brabourne Stadium and other academies, and was part of Under 16 and Under 19 summer camps. His batch included Prithvi Shaw, Sarfaraz Khan, Armaan Jaffer and Aman Khan. In the selection matches he was usually somewhere in the top 80 or 100. He never made the top 30 who became Mumbai probables.

The conversation with Abhishek Nayar

A personal coach near his home, who worked in the merchant navy and trained a handful of committed boys for free, introduced him to Abhishek Nayar when Tejas was about 20. Nayar watched them for a week and then took them into his academy. Through him Tejas bowled to Ajinkya Rahane, Rohit Sharma, Robin Uthappa and Shreyas Iyer at grounds around Mumbai, and saw at close range the fitness, routines and skill that cricket at that level demands.

In 2018, at the Mumbai Indians ground in Ghansoli, Nayar asked to talk to him. Tejas was 22, and Nayar told him honestly that the gap between his skills and the level he needed was very big. He gave him one more year, his last at Under 23 level. After that there are no age group trials, and players have to force their way into senior cricket through club and office matches. If nothing had happened by then, Tejas should switch, and Nayar offered two options: strength and conditioning or cricket coaching. Tejas answered immediately. "No strength and conditioning, I don't want to do squats and all that every time," he told him. He wanted to coach cricket. The game, he says, had given him his good habits and changed him as a person, and he felt he had something to give back.

Learning to coach

His coaching career began in mid 2019 under Nayar, in what he calls a paid internship. He soon found that playing and coaching were completely different. He read cricket books, took courses and watched hours of video, and he was on the ground from 7 in the morning until 8 or 9 at night, taking about one day off a month. At Nayar's suggestion he also worked at another well known Mumbai academy, so that he could see as many different players as possible.

Because he had never played at the top level, nothing came naturally. Parents and children would ask him what he had played. He had to learn skills that other coaches seemed to have already, such as giving throwdowns and running fielding sessions. Starting young helped. "You need time to fail," he says. "I started coaching at the age of 23, that's a very young age to start coaching I feel, but it also gave me an option to fail."

His parents had no cricket background and never came to watch him play. At first cricket was a hobby, and like most Indian parents they wanted him to keep up his studies too. What changed their minds was the change in him: the boy who was lazy and cranky about getting up for school would wake two hours earlier on his own to go to practice. They never pressed him about money, and he believes that freed him to focus on learning rather than on what he was earning.

He had other voices to deal with. As a child he struggled with obesity and was bullied and body shamed at school, and people told him a cricket career was impossible. He says those voices became a driving force. "When you train, when you run, you feel those voices in your ears, it goes in your head and you start pushing your body far more," he says.

Academies, a film and franchise cricket

Around 2020 and 2021 he ran his own academy, Divine Cricket Academy, for about a year, with two branches, in partnership with Ajinkya Kamlay, an experienced coach who taught him a great deal about the soft skills of the job. When Nayar set up All Art Academy in 2023, Tejas gave up his own academy to join it. Running his own place, he felt, would keep the money coming but stop him learning, and the game was changing fast, with T20 totals that once sat around 160 or 180 now reaching 220 or more.

That year Nayar gave him an unusual assignment. For two and a half years he was assistant coach on the film Mr. and Mrs. Mahi, working under a senior coach to help Janhvi Kapoor and Rajkummar Rao look and play like cricketers. They practised twice a day around shooting days of 12 hours or more, and seeing how hard the actors worked changed how he thought about film stars.

He has worked two WPL seasons with UP Warriors, in 2025 and 2026, the first under head coach Jon Lewis. He also scouts for Kolkata Knight Riders, following leagues around the country to find young players before anyone else notices them. In the T20 Mumbai League he worked with the SoBo Mumbai Falcons, first with the men's team and then, this year, with the women's team in the competition's first women's season. They won it. He calls it his first big win as a coach on a big platform.

What he wants now

Outside cricket he listens to music, writes shayari and calls himself a foodie. His short term goal is for his students to keep getting better, and for him to keep learning so that he can pass it on. In the long run he wants to see as many of them as possible play for Mumbai, in the IPL or for India, without repeating his mistakes. "I always feel that a coach is only as good as a player is," he says. Given unlimited money, he would build a large institution where underprivileged children could study and play several sports, not only cricket.
