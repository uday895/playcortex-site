Eshaan Sethi on Choosing College Over a Golf Career, and Helping Others Choose

"Sometimes I look back at it and I'm like maybe I made the right call, maybe I made the wrong call."

Eshaan Sethi now runs a college counselling company that helps young Indian athletes get recruited to play their sport at American universities. He calls himself a retired athlete, but the business grew directly out of his own years in junior golf and the decision he faced at 17.

He first went to the golf course at four, tagging along when his parents played their weekend rounds at the Delhi Golf Club. Until 11 or 12 he also played cricket and football, but by 10 or 11 he was ranked second or third in the country in his age group. He and his parents agreed it was time to take golf seriously. From 12 until he left for college at 17, he was ranked in the top five on the Indian National Junior Tour, and he was on track to turn professional.

Pinehurst at ten

When he was ten, his parents took him to the US Kids World Championship in Pinehurst. He was taken aback by the other boys, who were much bigger than him and hit the ball further. He finished around 55th or 60th out of 200 and was happy with that. After he came home he won a few national events. "That's when I realized that yeah maybe I do have it in me," he says. He was always a hard worker and loved being on the course.

At 16, just after his Class 10 board exams, he had been away from the game for two or three months. When he came back he trained too hard and tore tissue in the lower right side of his back, which kept him out for five or six months. Fortunately, the spine was not involved. He took it as a lesson: stretch before playing, and keep working on the muscles that let you down. "Every time you have an injury, it's like a learning lesson for you," he says.

The decision at 17

When American colleges began recruiting him, he had offers from Division 1 and Division 3 programmes. He chose a school with an acceptance rate below 8 percent over one with a stronger golf programme. "I was not going to get into that school without my golf," he says. "So I kind of used my golf to get into a good academic program."

His parents supported him, but they also laid out the practical case for a degree from a prestigious university, and his college counsellor at the time guided him through the choice. Money was a large part of it. In a team sport, he points out, the team pays for hotels and food. "In golf you're a freelancer, you have to pay for everything." In a country where sports other than cricket struggle for sponsors, a golfer's prize money mostly goes on the cost of playing. If the pay had been better, he says, he would definitely have considered turning professional.

He still is not sure he chose correctly. "It was one of the most difficult decisions I've taken," he says. "Sometimes I look back at it and I'm like maybe I made the right call, maybe I made the wrong call." With the stronger golf programme he might have given the professional game a chance. Now he watches it from outside, as some of his peers do well and others do not.

Same seat, same putts

He played college golf for four years, though golf was no longer the priority it had been at 16 and 17. As a junior his mother had travelled with him everywhere. In college he travelled with the team and built a fixed routine. He always sat at the back on the left side of the team van, and everyone knew it was his seat. At the course he foam rolled and stretched, hit 25 to 40 balls on the range, chipped a little, went back to hit longer clubs, and finished with a putting drill: two balls three feet from the hole, five, ten or fifteen putts, just to get used to the ball dropping in. Once he started playing well he would not change any of it. "I was extremely superstitious when I used to play," he says.

After a round there was little time for recovery beyond food and an ice bath back at the hotel. He did not work out during tournaments because it left him sore, and saved strength work for the off season. He worked with a strength and conditioning coach, a swing coach and a physiotherapist. Looking at the athletes he advises now, he thinks the best young players are adding mental coaches, and he believes that if you are strong, positive and present in the moment, half your problems are solved.

Building the company

He graduated with a degree in economics, came home and joined KPMG India. In October last year, while still working there, he started the counselling company with a business partner who also played college golf and is now a professional. Juniors in golf had been asking Eshaan for advice on recruitment, and he had found it hard, when he was going through it himself, to find counsellors who understood sports like golf and tennis. He decided to build a business around the gap. Counselling suited him better than coaching, he says, because very few Indians have played college golf or tennis in the United States, while coaching is already crowded with former professionals.

What began as a side project now works with about a dozen athletes. Eleven are golfers and one plays squash, and the company has begun taking clients from other sports, along with a division for mentoring and development. Eshaan resigned from KPMG to run it full time, and he wants to make it one of the biggest college counselling companies for athletes in the country. He credits sport, and an individual sport in particular, with giving him the confidence to do it. "I like to make my own decisions because that's what I've been doing," he says.

Golf also shaped how he works. The game taught him to take one shot at a time rather than fix on the final score, and he started the business focused on the process rather than the result. When he signed his first client he was very happy, and also felt the weight of responsibility for getting that player into a good college. He says he tries to give every client 150 percent, as he tried to do in practice as a player. He has seen what that kind of attitude looks like from the other side. One 16 year old he works with injured his wrist at a tournament in the United States and had a poor first day. Eshaan told him to withdraw. The boy played on and shot the best score of the tournament on the second day, then kept that form going on the third.

His advice

The trend he argues against most strongly is young athletes leaving school or moving to open schooling at 13 or 14. Seven of the twelve athletes he works with are in open schooling, and more are asking whether they should be. He thinks that age is far too early to make the decision. At 16 and 17 he wanted to turn professional and knew nothing else, and only later did he realise it was not the best path for him. He would rather athletes stay in school and ask their schools for flexibility with holidays, exam dates and classes.

He also believes every athlete needs a plan B, and he sees college sport in the United States as one: four years competing against strong amateurs, and a degree that can lead to a job if an injury or the economics of the sport end a career early. "No matter how hard you work if you're not thinking on your feet in the moment," he says, "things can go anywhere."

His company takes athletes on at about 15 and works with them for two or three years before recruitment. That is the age, he says, when a young player should start building a profile for college sport.
