Rishit Khanna

Rishit Khanna and the tournament in Chandigarh that kept him playing

"That's the core memory I'll never forget."

Rishit Khanna came to badminton by a roundabout route. He played cricket first, at state level, and then tennis, before his father suggested an individual sport because there was a good badminton academy nearby. He did not take to it at once. "Initially I didn't enjoy it much," he says. Around the age of ten he gave it a proper try, and the interest grew from there. Nearly ten years later he has two CBSE national medals and an All India ranking.

His first tournament, an Under 11 event, ended with a silver medal, and with it came attention and talk of his potential. Then came three years in which he did not get past the first round anywhere. His parents kept sending him to evening sessions and weekend fitness training, and their message stayed the same: play for fun and do not get caught up in the pressure while you are young. It was only when he won bronze at the Under 19 State Championship that he understood badminton had become more than a hobby.

A thousand entries, 32 places

What stayed with him from those years was how competitive the sport is. In an Under 19 national ranking tournament, a single category can draw a thousand entries, and only 32 reach the main draw, which is the only way to earn a ranking at all. Over about five years he played five or six of these tournaments and kept going out in the third, fourth or fifth round, each time without anyone noticing.

By class 12 he was training six to seven hours a day across three sessions while studying physics, chemistry and maths for the engineering entrance exams. He was ready to stop. He gave himself one last attempt, a national ranking tournament in Chandigarh. If it went well he would carry on; if not, he would concentrate on his studies for a year and think again.

He qualified for the main draw, and he still calls it his biggest achievement. He and his doubles partner beat players who were already representing India internationally, including a singles player ranked in the top three. Their parents had already packed the car, expecting a loss. "When we came out and said we'd won, nobody could believe it," he says. "That's the core memory I'll never forget." The feeling of those matches has stayed with him too. "That competitiveness, the nerves before a match, your heart racing, is something you can't forget," he says. "Winning under that kind of pressure is addictive in a way nothing else is."

Studies and sport

He does not believe in an even split between school and sport. The balance, as he sees it, shifts year by year and depends on what works for the individual. He chose science in class 11 expecting to manage both easily, and found out quickly that he could not. He stopped chasing the toughest engineering entrances and leaned towards badminton, giving it roughly 60 percent of his time that year and keeping up with studies through online coaching and tuition.

Soon after he earned his All India ranking, he and his father took an honest look at how competitive the top level was and decided against pursuing it full time. The balance swung the other way, first to 80 percent studies and then 90, through JEE and his board exams. Now in college, he plays again, mostly for enjoyment, with a sports scholarship that funds two tournaments a year. He still hopes to compete seriously again.

For five or six years his days followed the same pattern: school, the academy from three to six, home, dinner, an hour of study and sleep. There was almost no time for friends, and he does not regret it. He also held off joining Instagram until after class 12 to protect his schedule. He is careful, though, about what he credits to the sport. "Discipline ultimately comes down to mindset, not the sport itself," he says. What badminton gave him was the structure that made discipline easier to keep.

His father

Asked who shaped his journey most, he names his father without hesitating. Rishit is frank that he reacted badly to losses in the past, including breaking rackets, and he admits that in his father's place he might have told a child to stop. His father stayed calm instead, corrected him gently when it was needed, and kept telling him to enjoy the game and believe in his own path. He drove Rishit to sessions straight from work and travelled with him to tournament after tournament. He has never been able to watch his son's matches, because he finds them too nervous to sit through. He waits outside and only checks in if a match runs unusually long.

Close matches

Pressure, for Rishit, arrives at particular moments: falling behind early as errors pile up, or a tight finish at 17 all or 18 all. He has lost a match after leading 14 to 8, and won one after trailing 17 to 14. In those moments he stops looking at the crowd and concentrates on believing in himself. "That's the only real strategy you have," he says.

Away from the court he reads widely, practises japa regularly and is interested in how the mind works. He also thinks badminton is badly marketed: an entertaining, intense sport that people overlook because they do not know when tournaments are on.

What he wants most from the sport is not a personal result. "The thing I want most is impact," he says. He would like someone to watch him struggle, still give everything, and leave motivated in their own life, which is why a loss can sometimes matter more to him than a win. He points to Manraj Singh, a player from Faridabad who trained at Prakash Padukone's academy and now competes internationally, as the example that made that level feel reachable.

His advice to his younger self is to enjoy the sport as much as possible. "Losing isn't the end of the world, and winning isn't everything either. Trust the process and just be in the moment." For now, his aim is to balance engineering and badminton and enjoy both. Further ahead, his ambition is a spiritual composure that keeps him steady. In five words he calls himself disciplined, hardworking, principled, emotional and a little nervous, and he adds that he takes his losses hard.
