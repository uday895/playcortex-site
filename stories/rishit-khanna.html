Rishit Khanna

The Ratio That Kept Shifting

"Winning under that kind of pressure is addictive in a way nothing else is."

Rishit Khanna's path to badminton wasn't direct. He started with cricket, playing at the state level, then moved to tennis, before his father suggested he try an individual sport, since there was a good academy nearby. The switch wasn't love at first sight. "Initially I didn't enjoy it much," he says, but around age 10 he gave it a real try, and the interest slowly built from there. Nearly ten years later, he has two CBSE national medals and an All India ranking to show for it.

The Sport's Brutal Arithmetic

What stayed with him most from those early years was simply how competitive the sport was. In an under-19 national ranking tournament, a single category might see a thousand entries, with only 32 making the main draw, the only ones who earn a rank at all. Over about five years, he played five or six ranking tournaments and kept losing in the third, fourth, or fifth round, unnoticed each time. By class 12, training six to seven hours a day across three sessions while also grinding through PCM coursework for engineering entrances, he was ready to walk away.

He gave himself one last shot, a national ranking tournament in Chandigarh. If it worked out, he'd keep going; if not, he'd focus on studies for a year and reassess. He qualified. "That competitiveness, the nerves before a match, your heart racing, is something you can't forget," he says. "Winning under that kind of pressure is addictive in a way nothing else is."

No Perfect Balance

Managing school and sport together, he says, was never about finding an even split. "People talk about 50-50, but really it's 60-40, 70-30, whatever works for you." He chose PCM in 11th grade expecting to manage both easily, and reality corrected that assumption fast. He gave up chasing the toughest engineering entrances to lean into the sport instead, a 60-40 split in badminton's favour that year, supplemented by online coaching and tuitions. But right after earning his All India ranking, he and his father looked honestly at how competitive the top tier really was, and decided against chasing it full-time. The ratio flipped, 80-20, then eventually 90-10, in favour of studies through JEE and boards. Now in college, he's playing again, mostly for enjoyment, backed by a sports scholarship and full funding for two tournaments a year, though he still holds out hope of competing seriously again.

The Medal That Made It Real

His first tournament, an under-11 event, ended in a silver medal, and with it came outside attention and belief in his potential. What followed was three years without qualifying past the first round anywhere. Even so, his parents kept letting him attend evening sessions and weekend fitness training, always framing it the same way: play for fun, don't get too caught up in the pressure while you're young. It wasn't until he won bronze at the Under 19 State Championship that he says he truly understood badminton had become more than a hobby.

The achievement he calls his biggest, though, came later: qualifying for the national main draw in Chandigarh, where he and his doubles partner beat opponents already representing India internationally, including a top-three ranked singles player. Their parents had already packed the car, assuming a loss. "When we came out and said we'd won, nobody could believe it," he says. "That's the core memory I'll never forget."

His Father, In the Background

Ask him who shaped this journey most, and the answer is immediate: his father. Rishit is candid about having had "ugly reactions" after losses in the past, including broken rackets, moments he isn't proud of. In his father's position, he admits, he might have told a kid to stop. Instead, his father stayed calm, offered gentle correction when needed, and kept repeating the same message: enjoy the game, believe in your path. He drove Rishit to sessions straight from work without resting and travelled with him to tournament after tournament, providing an emotional steadiness Rishit credits directly for keeping him in the sport. Notably, his father has never been able to watch his matches, finding it too nerve-wracking, and would instead wait outside, checking in only if a match ran unusually long.

Learning to Believe

His first taste of recognition came earlier, in cricket, around age six or seven, when an older student asked his father if he could take a photo with Rishit after hearing about him. That early moment, he says, was when he first felt like he might be building something real.

The years that followed reshaped his social life more than anything else: school, then straight to the academy from 3 to 6, home, dinner, an hour of study, and sleep, a routine that held for five or six years with almost no time for friends. He doesn't regret it. "I think most serious athletes go through some version of that trade-off," he says. As for whether it built discipline, he's careful to separate the two. "Discipline ultimately comes down to mindset, not the sport itself," he says, though he credits sport with providing the structure that makes discipline easier to sustain.

Pressure, Tested and Retested

The pressure of a match, for him, becomes real in specific moments: falling behind early as errors pile up, or a tight endgame at 17-all, 18-all. He's lost a match after leading 14-8, and come back to win one after trailing 17-14. In those moments, he says, you stop watching the crowd entirely and focus only on believing in yourself. "That's the only real strategy you have."

Outside the sport, he reads widely and has grown drawn to spirituality, practising japa regularly and following closely how the mind works psychologically. He held off getting Instagram until after 12th grade specifically to protect his schedule. He also thinks badminton itself is under-marketed as a sport, entertaining and intense, yet often overlooked simply because people don't know when tournaments are happening.

What Keeps Him Playing

Above all, what he wants from the sport isn't a personal result. "The thing I want most is impact," he says, for someone to watch him struggle and still give everything, and walk away motivated to push themselves in their own life. Even a loss, in that light, can matter more than a win, if it moves someone else forward. He points to Manraj Singh, a player from Faridabad training at Prakash Padukone's academy who now competes internationally, as the kind of example that made this level feel achievable to him.

Asked what he'd tell his younger self, his answer is simple: enjoy the sport as much as you can. "Losing isn't the end of the world, and winning isn't everything either. Trust the process and just be in the moment." His short-term goal is straightforward, balancing engineering and badminton and enjoying both. Long term, his ambition tilts inward, toward a level of spiritual composure that keeps him steady and undistracted. Asked to sum himself up in five words, he lands on disciplined, hardworking, principled, emotional, and a little nervous, adding, without hesitation, that he takes his losses hard and isn't afraid to admit it.
