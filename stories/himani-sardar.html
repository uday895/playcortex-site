Himani Sardar

Himani Sardar, from Siri Fort to the squash court

"I like myself more when I'm on the court, even when I'm not winning, than when I'm just in class or hanging out."

Himani Sardar first played racket sports in a park near her home in Delhi, in casual games with her father. A passing remark from one of his gym clients, whose daughter played tennis at an academy, led him to Siri Fort. Himani enrolled there at eight or nine under coach Dinesh Khanna. Within two years the coaches had invited her father, who until then had watched from the sidelines with the other parents, to join as a trainer.

In 2016 she moved to the badminton and squash academy at Siri Fort under Madhumita Bisht, a Padma Shri awardee and one of the most respected coaches in the country. Her father moved with her. When Himani met Bisht for the first time, she touched her feet out of nervous respect, as if meeting a celebrity. By 2017 she was training twice a day and attending school for little more than exams.

The body type remark

In 2018, at a state championship, one of her coaches told her father that Himani did not have the body type of a sportsperson and lacked the stamina and physicality for it. Her father had already invested a great deal of time and money, and he did not give up. He gave her three months, changed her diet and added extra training and a second coach. She came second in the championship, which surprised everyone. "I saw how happy my dad was, and I understood the struggle behind it," she says, "the money he'd borrowed from people just so I could train and compete. That was the moment I realised I could actually do something with this."

She reached the quarterfinals of the Under 13 Nationals in Imphal, Manipur, finishing in the top eight in the country and beating the player ranked sixth in India along the way. Nobody had expected a player from Delhi to get that far. The family did not have money for the journey home, so her prize of five thousand rupees paid for the tickets.

Her father as coach

The years that followed asked a lot of her. From about the age of twelve she took buses to training alone early in the morning and added solo sessions with her father on top of academy work. There was little time for school friends. For six or seven years she called her father "sir" during training. "When we came home, that familiarity you'd normally expect between a parent and child just wasn't there in the same way," she says. She remembers a toffee wrapper falling out of her pocket during a warm up, and his anger at the broken diet. For a while afterwards she felt distant from him.

2019

She calls 2019 her best and worst year. She made the Under 15 state squad and was selected for the national squad, but injured her patella just before the tournament. She cried for hours the week before, sure she would have to withdraw, and played anyway. She reached the final and became so exhausted during it that officials stopped play to let her recover. She won 21 to 19 and became the top ranked player in her age group. Two months later, at the Nationals in singles and doubles, the same injury kept her from playing her best singles. The lockdown began soon after.

She names two people as the biggest influences on her career. Madhumita ma'am pushed her on and off the court. Coach Vikram sir taught her strictly technical badminton but carried much of the emotional weight of her journey. After one hard quarterfinal win at a state championship, she walked out of the stadium to find him waiting just to see her, and he hugged her there in the middle of the road. It is one of the memories she values most.

Losing the sport

Her way of dealing with a loss is to cry in private, take two or three hours alone and then analyse what went wrong. "The real fight, when I'm on court, isn't against my opponent, it's against myself, my own mental and physical state," she says. She describes it as a lonely path, with no one there in the middle of the night to reassure you.

Her last major badminton setback came in a semifinal at the Delhi State Championship against an opponent ranked in the top 30. She led the first game 17 to 10 before an old, untreated injury flared up in her shoulder and upper body. She lost, and with it the chance to play the final of her last state championship before college.

She had worked with a nutritionist early on, during her time as the top ranked player in her age group, but stopped afterwards. She was later diagnosed with Female Athlete Triad, which she says came from seeking help too late, after the condition had already set in and been made worse by the lockdown. It contributed to her decline and, at one point, to thoughts of giving up badminton. "I had good coaching and training, but nutrition was the piece I neglected," she says. She also wishes she had seen a sports psychologist earlier, and believes every athlete should have access to one in their peak years.

When injury eventually kept her off the badminton court, she went through a real crisis of identity, because she had always thought of herself simply as a badminton player. Squash gave her a way back, and she later came third at the Nationals in that sport. "I don't miss badminton itself as much as I miss who I was when I was playing it," she says. "I like myself more when I'm on the court, even when I'm not winning, than when I'm just in class or hanging out."

Now

These days the difficulty is more mental than physical. She still goes to practice and the gym, but worry about jobs, internships and her career pulls at her focus. Her next goal is to finish first at the University Nationals trials next year, having come third this time. Longer term she wants a stable career without giving up sport. Her advice to her younger self: "Don't be so hard on yourself. It's okay, things happen, life happens, and it's not worth stressing over every single thing." In five words she calls herself hardworking, open minded, chill, resilient and determined.
