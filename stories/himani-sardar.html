Himani Sardar

The Version of Herself She Plays To Find

"I don't miss badminton itself as much as I miss who I was when I was playing it."

Himani Sardar's introduction to racket sports started as something ordinary: casual games with her father in the park near their Delhi home, their family's roots tracing back to Mumbai. It was a passing comment from one of her father's gym clients, whose daughter played tennis at an academy, that led him to Siri Fort. Himani enrolled there around age eight or nine under coach Dinesh Khanna, and within two years her father, who had simply been watching from the sidelines like the other parents, was invited by the coaches to join as a trainer himself.

Finding Madhumita Ma'am

In 2016, she shifted to the badminton and squash academy at Siri Fort under Madhumita Bisht, a Padma Shri awardee considered one of the best coaches in the country. Her father moved with her again. Meeting Bisht for the first time left such an impression that Himani, out of nervous respect, touched her feet, as though meeting a celebrity. By 2017, she was doing double sessions and had all but stopped attending school beyond exams.

The turning point came in 2018. At a state championship, one of her coaches told her father bluntly that Himani didn't have the body type typically associated with sportspeople, that she lacked the stamina and physicality for it. Her father, already having invested heavily in time and money, didn't give up. He gave her three months, overhauled her diet, and added extra training and a sub coach. She came second in that championship, surprising everyone. "I saw how happy my dad was, and I understood the struggle behind it," she says, "the money he'd borrowed from people just so I could train and compete. That was the moment I realised I could actually do something with this."

A Complicated Kind of Closeness

The years that followed asked a great deal of her. She began waking early and taking buses to training alone from around age twelve, layering solo sessions with her father on top of her regular academy work. There was little time left for school friendships. And there was a quieter cost too: she called her father "sir" during training for six or seven years. "When we came home, that familiarity you'd normally expect between a parent and child just wasn't there in the same way," she says. She remembers a toffee wrapper falling from her pocket during a warm up, and her father's fury at the broken diet, a small moment that left her feeling emotionally distant from him for a while.

The Injury, the Final, the Nationals

2019 was, by her own description, her best and worst year. She made her Under 15 state squad and got selected for the national squad, but a patella injury struck just before the tournament. She cried for hours the week before, convinced she'd have to withdraw, but played anyway. She reached the final, grew so exhausted mid-match that officials stopped play so she could recover, and won 21-19, becoming the number one ranked player in her age group. Two months later, at Nationals in both singles and doubles, the same lingering injury kept her from performing at her best in singles. The lockdown began right after.

The Quarterfinal in Imphal

A few months after her first big tournament result, she travelled to the Under 13 Nationals in Imphal, Manipur, reaching the quarterfinals and finishing in the top eight in the country, beating the number six ranked player in India along the way. Nobody had expected her to get that far representing Delhi. The detail she still remembers clearly: the family didn't have money for the return trip, so her five thousand rupees in prize money went straight into the tickets home.

Two People, Two Kinds of Support

Asked who shaped her journey most, she names two people: Madhumita ma'am, who pushed her both on and off the court, and coach Vikram sir, who carried the emotional weight of the journey even though his coaching itself was strictly technical. She remembers one moment vividly: after a tough quarterfinal win, walking out of the stadium to find him waiting just to see her, and being hugged right there in the middle of the road. It remains one of her most precious memories, from what turned out to be her last state championship.

Losing, Alone and Otherwise

Setbacks, for her, follow a pattern: cry privately, take two or three hours alone, then move into self analysis. "The real fight, when I'm on court, isn't against my opponent, it's against myself, my own mental and physical state," she says. She calls the journey a lonely one, since no one is there in the middle of the night to reassure you. Her most recent significant setback came in a semifinal at the Delhi State Championship against a top-30 ranked opponent, where she led the first set 17-10 before an old, unaddressed injury flared into her shoulder and upper body. She lost the match, missing what would have been the final of her last state championship before college.

Losing the Sport, and Finding It Again

When injury eventually kept her off the badminton court, she went through what she describes as a real identity crisis, having thought of herself simply as "a badminton player." Taking up squash, and later coming third in Nationals in that sport, gave her a way to reclaim that identity in a new form. "I don't miss badminton itself as much as I miss who I was when I was playing it," she says. "I like myself more when I'm on the court, even when I'm not winning, than when I'm just in class or hanging out."

The Piece She Regrets Neglecting

She had a nutritionist early in her career, during her run as the top-ranked player in her age group, but stopped working with one afterward. She was later diagnosed with Female Athlete Triad, a diagnosis she's careful to note came from seeking help too late, well after the condition had already begun, compounded by the lockdown. It became a real factor in her career dipping, and at one point, in considering quitting badminton altogether. "I had good coaching and training, but nutrition was the piece I neglected," she says, adding that she wishes she'd also seen a sports psychologist earlier, something she believes every athlete should have access to during their peak years.

Where She Is Now

These days, the struggle is less physical and more mental. Practice and the gym remain on her schedule, but stress about upcoming jobs, internships, and her career has pulled at her focus, even as she keeps showing up. Her short-term goal is finishing first at University Nationals trials next year, having come third this time. Longer term, she wants a stable career without giving up sport altogether. Asked what she'd tell her younger self, she doesn't hesitate: "Don't be so hard on yourself. It's okay, things happen, life happens, and it's not worth stressing over every single thing." In five words, she is hardworking, open minded, chill, resilient, and determined, still working out who she is outside of the sport that has shaped so much of who she is within it.
