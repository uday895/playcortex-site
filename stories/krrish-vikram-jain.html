Krrish Vikram Jain

Krrish Vikram Jain and the five months after his ACL tear

"During that five months I kept my head down, I kept working on myself, I kept my physio going every day."

Krrish Vikram Jain first kicked a football properly on the Harvard Business School ground. It was 2014 or 2015, his family was visiting the United States, and it was his elder brother who got him playing. Until then, like most Indian children, he had played cricket. Today he is vice captain of the Ashoka University football team, a centre back who expects to captain the side in his fourth year.

Under 16 football at ten

In 2016 he started formal coaching at his school in Vasant Vihar, New Delhi, under the coach he still credits with teaching him everything from the basics upwards. He began in central midfield and went on to play almost every position, even keeping goal for the school in penalty shootouts. "When I started taking coaching I realised that I think this is the sport for me," he says.

That same year he joined Bhaichung Bhutia Football Schools, one of the best known grassroots academies in Delhi. He was ten and playing in the Under 10 and Under 9 groups, but within his first month the coaches moved him up to play with the Under 16s. He considers himself lucky to have been introduced to competitive football so young. In 2017 he joined the BBFS Delhi development squad, which brings together the best players from the academy's centres.

Barcelona and Portugal

At 12, after trials across the academy, he was chosen for a BBFS team that went to Spain, and he won a full scholarship that paid for his flights and hotel. Playing as a striker at a club tournament in Barcelona, he finished as the third highest scorer and the team came third. His parents simply told him to go and enjoy himself. It was the first time anyone in the family had gone abroad so young to play sport, and the pressure he felt came from within. "I think I needed to repay back that faith, that confidence that they showed in me," he says.

In 2018 he went to Portugal, where he played league football for the first time, as well as beach football. The team did not finish in the top places, but European scouts asked his coaches about him. For family reasons he did not stay, and he has no regrets. If he had stayed and not made it as a professional, he says, he might have been left with nothing to fall back on, because education there came second to football.

In 2019, at 13, he went to the open trials for the Delhi Under 15 team. The selectors preferred 15 year olds in their final year at that level, but they wrote down his name and one or two others for the next season. That season was 2020, and Covid stopped it. BBFS kept its players going with online sessions every other day, full of fitness work, juggling and ball mastery in whatever small room or ground they had. Along the way he also played Under 13 football for BBFS and Under 15 football for AU Rajasthan FC.

Captain at school

When football returned in 2022, he was in Class 10 with board exams ahead. In Class 11 he captained his school to the CBSE cluster championship, a knockout competition for CBSE schools in Delhi that around 140 to 150 schools entered. It was the school's first tournament win in 50 years. The team went on to represent Delhi at the CBSE nationals in Bhiwadi, Rajasthan, and went out, as he remembers it, in the quarterfinals. It was his first experience of playing against teams from other states.

In Class 12, in 2024, his school won the zonal and interzonal championships, and he was selected as a centre back for the Delhi team at the state school games. The squad was a week away from travelling to Jammu and Kashmir when, after talking with his parents, he withdrew to concentrate on his Class 12 exams. He calls it a little heartbreaking but has no regrets. He did well in his exams and got into good universities, and he believes football helped. "The colleges that I got into, 90% of those saw football on my resume and said that yes, this guy can manage things, he's a multitasker," he says. He credits the game with teaching him perseverance, discipline and leadership. He has captained his school and, he says, a Delhi team too.

The ACL

On 21 September 2024, in his first year at university, he completely tore his anterior cruciate ligament during a college match and was rushed to hospital. It was the first injury of his life. Before that he had not had so much as a sprain or a muscle spasm. He had surgery on 10 October, could barely move for about a month afterwards, and took his first steps with a walker.

He describes what followed as a mental battle as much as a physical one. People asked whether he would get back to his level and worried that he would be injured again, and he began to doubt himself too. "During that five months I kept my head down, I kept working on myself, I kept my physio going every day," he says. He held on to one goal, getting back to 100 percent, and his parents and friends kept telling him he would.

He was back training with the university team on 15 March 2025, about five months after surgery. On 27 March the team played the annual sports fest at BITS Goa and won it. It was his first tournament for the college, and he started at centre back. He says it was the first time in the college's history that a freshman had started in his first tournament. Later in 2025 he was named vice captain, and the team won six of its eight tournaments, beating the Inter DU champions twice.

Outside football

Krrish has always kept bad games to himself. On the way home from a poor match or session, he goes over what went wrong and what he would do differently. He also talks to his coaches, whom his parents taught him to treat with great respect. His friends have been the other constant. "Till date, they tell me that, bro, you've got it in you," he says.

The most draining part of his early years, he says, was coming home exhausted from training and still having to finish his schoolwork, a pressure no one put on him but himself. As a BBFS player he followed the academy's diet charts, and his advice on strength and conditioning is practical. If you play four times a week, set aside one day for a planned session, from warm up to core work, and have a coach look over the plan. Nutrition, he adds, matters as much as training.

Away from the pitch, he says his life has been governed by two Fs, football and finance. He has followed the markets since he was 16, first through his father's account and now through his own, and his internships include one at HDFC Asset Management Company. He has travelled to more than 20 countries and around 20 Indian states. He supports Manchester City. His idols are Cristiano Ronaldo, for his work ethic, and Sergio Ramos, for the way he led a Real Madrid side full of stars.

He is now going into his third year, preparing for the GMAT and planning a one year postgraduate degree in finance, most likely abroad or at ISB. With unlimited money, he says, he would build a state of the art football training facility in India to give something back to the game. His longer term wish is closer to home: to give his parents, especially his mother, a comfortable retirement.
