Shreya Sharma, the goalkeeper who learned a goal is never hers alone

"It can't just be my mistake if a goal goes in."

Shreya Sharma first played football in the big break at Sanskriti School in Delhi, a twenty minute recess meant for rest. While most of the girls spent it with friends, she joined about fifteen boys in the central courtyard of the junior school for five a side games with a small "smiley ball". Years later she would keep goal for Delhi and be called up to the India team.

The school had a strong football culture, but mostly for boys. Sports teachers handed the footballs to the boys and steered the girls towards basketball.

A sporting family with other plans

Sport ran through her family. Her father and uncle played national level tennis, and her grandfather played football and cricket. Each side of the family had its own idea of what she should play. Her maternal grandparents enrolled her in basketball, which they thought was more "girls-friendly" and "less physical", and she played it seriously enough to reach the Delhi state level. Her father's side preferred individual sports such as golf or tennis, so that she would not have to "rely on a team to play well". When it was clear that football was what she loved, her parents hired a coach to train her one to one in their colony park, though the sessions were mostly fitness.

Delhi, and the blame

In sixth grade she was noticed in house matches and joined the school's combined middle and senior team. Two seniors mentored her: a goalkeeper who had played for India, and a midfielder who was vice captain. In eighth grade, at twelve or thirteen, she made the Delhi state team. It was the moment she realised she could take football seriously, because she had reached the same level as the most successful athletes in her family.

Her first Nationals were hard. Delhi held Bihar, one of the top teams, goalless until the 75th minute of a 90 minute match, then fell apart in the last fifteen minutes and lost by four goals. Against Manipur, the best side in the country, they were level at one goal each for much of the game before losing two goals to one.

Afterwards the coaches singled her out as the goalkeeper, and she took the blame on herself. She came to believe that "if the goalkeeper concedes the goal then only we lose". It was worse at a boys only talent camp for Delhi Dynamos, where boys would say, "Oh, she's a girl. Don't put her on our team," and her gender was blamed for every mistake. "As a goalkeeper, our mistakes get amplified a lot more and they're a lot more visible."

Stepping away and coming back

The criticism and the bias left her anxious about playing, and during the Covid lockdown she stepped away from football. She put her energy into Model United Nations and debating instead. Towards the end of tenth grade she realised she wanted to play again and joined a club in the Delhi Women's League. A supportive coach there helped her recover her game sense, and she worked at accepting that the ball has to get past ten other players before it reaches the goalkeeper. "It can't just be my mistake if a goal goes in."

In eleventh grade her family suggested she concentrate on her studies, since most players make the national team by fifteen. Her answer was, "You never know if I keep trying, maybe I might make it." That year she was called up to the India team.

Still playing

In twelfth grade she chose academics over a professional football career, pointing to the lack of infrastructure and government support for women's football in India. She is now a second year economics student at Lady Shri Ram College, still plays for the Delhi University team and intends to keep playing for Delhi at the national level. She also plays guitar and piano, hikes, goes rock climbing and still plays golf with her father. "I try to just be passionate in whatever I am doing... whether it's economics or whether it's football."

Before a match she pictures herself saving goals, taking goal kicks and giving good commands, and she gives herself a pep talk in the mirror. She listens to FIFA World Cup songs and the title track from Dangal to get into a "fighting mind", and she leads a huddle so that her teammates do not get angry with each other over mistakes. She admits she feels more pressure in smaller matches than in big ones, "because... you just feel like you have more to prove." The advice she returns to most is her mother's: "Just focus on the ball... take every ball as it comes."

What she went through in goal now helps her elsewhere. Facing exams, she tells herself, "If I could conquer that mountain, then I can definitely get through this."
