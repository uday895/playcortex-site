Shreya Sharma

Breaking Barriers, One Save at a Time

"It's just a game at the end of the day."

Goalkeepers carry a different kind of pressure, because every mistake is magnified and every hesitation is visible. That intensity is exactly what drew Shreya Sharma to the position.

The Courtyard Footballer

She was at Sanskriti School in Delhi from nursery to twelfth grade. The school loved football, but mainly for boys. Boys were handed footballs and girls were steered towards basketball. In the 20-minute recess, Shreya joined the boys in the central courtyard for 5v5 games with a small "smiley ball."

Her family was sporty and protective. Her maternal grandparents signed her up for basketball because they saw it as more "girls-friendly," and she reached Delhi state level in it. Her father's side preferred individual sports such as golf or tennis, where she wouldn't have to "rely on a team." When it was clear football was what she loved, her parents found her a one-on-one coach in the colony park.

Delhi, Then the Weight of Blame

She was spotted in house matches in sixth grade and joined the school team. Two seniors mentored her, one of them a goalkeeper who had played for India. By eighth grade she was in the Delhi state team.

At her first Nationals, Delhi held Bihar 0-0 until the 75th minute and then lost 4-0. Against Manipur they were level at 1-1 before losing 2-1. Afterwards the coaches singled her out. She began to believe that "if the goalkeeper concedes the goal, then only we lose." At a boys-only Delhi Dynamos talent camp, she heard "Oh, she's a girl. Don't put her on our team."

Stepping Away, Coming Back

During the Covid lockdown she stepped away from football and threw herself into Model UN and debating. Near the end of tenth grade she realised she missed playing and joined a club in the Delhi Women's League. A supportive coach helped her rebuild her game sense, and she learned a simple truth: "It can't just be my mistake if a goal goes in."

In eleventh grade her family suggested she focus on her studies, since most players make the national side by fifteen. "You never know if I keep trying, maybe I might make it." She was called up to the India team that year.

Every Ball as It Comes

In twelfth grade she chose academics over a professional career, pointing to the lack of infrastructure and support for women's football in India. She is now studying economics at Lady Shri Ram College and still plays for Delhi University.

Before games she pictures herself making saves and giving clear commands. She gives herself a pep talk in the mirror and plays the Dangal title track. Her mother's advice still guides her: "Just focus on the ball... take every ball as it comes."

"If I could conquer that mountain, then I can definitely get through this."
