Shekhar Jaiswal

The Boy Who Refused to Quit

"Results don't stay for long if there isn't a proper plan behind the work."

Some journeys begin with a dream. Others begin almost by accident. For Shekhar, it was neither a grand plan nor a childhood ambition to become a professional tennis player. It began when he was seven years old, in Aurangabad, now Chhatrapati Sambhaji Nagar, Maharashtra. On his way to play cricket, something caught his attention: a tennis club. He stopped and watched people hitting a ball across a net. He was fascinated, and that simple moment changed the direction of his life.

Through a connection of his uncle, he got the chance to train at the club. For the next six or seven years, tennis was simply something he loved. He didn't know about national rankings or AITA tournaments, or what it would take to become a professional. He simply played.

The Moment Everything Changed

Around Class 10, his world began to expand. Representing his school at a state-level DSO inter-school tournament at Andheri Sports Club, he won a match 9-1 and people began noticing his game. Then, still unranked, he beat a player ranked around No. 36 in India in the Under-18 category. Suddenly the people around him were asking a different question: what if this boy really has something special?

For Shekhar, the answer was not found in talent alone. It was found in work. He moved to the MSLTA centre in Aurangabad and began taking tennis seriously. But there was a problem many young athletes don't talk about enough: money. Tournament fees, travel, hotels, food, coaching, fitness, physiotherapy and equipment add up fast, and for a middle-class family every tournament became a financial calculation.

A Mother's Belief

Behind him stood the strongest pillar of his journey: his mother. Shekhar says that whatever he is today is because of her, because she never left him alone. His father, a bank manager, naturally wanted him to focus on education and a secure future. His mother saw a boy who had found something he truly loved, and chose to stand beside him.

When Shekhar began travelling across India for tournaments, she travelled with him. Sometimes they had only two bags between them, one with clothes and another with a rice maker, a roti maker and ingredients so they could cook their own food. There were times money was so tight that they slept at railway stations, woke up, freshened up and went straight to the court. No luxury, no entourage, no support team. Just a mother and son chasing a dream. When relatives asked why so much was being spent on tennis, and some said he would never achieve anything from it, she stayed.

When Winning Stopped

The hardest part of his journey wasn't losing a match. It was losing faith in himself. After turning 18 and moving into the men's category against older, more experienced players, the results simply didn't come for nearly three years. He would win one match and lose the next, sometimes losing the opening round itself. He travelled from Aurangabad to Chandigarh, Sonipat and Surat only to lose and return almost immediately. Thursday: travel. Friday: practice. Saturday: lose. Sunday: return.

Eventually he began asking himself whether he should quit. He sat alone in his room trying to understand what was wrong, and spoke to physiotherapists, his parents and a psychiatrist. He describes that period as a kind of depression, one he believes many athletes go through. Tennis can make it harder, because on court you are alone. Nobody can take the next shot for you.

3,000 Skips

Then came coach Arshad Warsi, young, enthusiastic and willing to work with Shekhar despite his financial limits. More than teaching tennis, he rebuilt Shekhar's discipline, mindset and routine. The schedule was brutal: up around 3:30 in the morning, on court by 4:30, training until 7 or 7:30, then classes and engineering preparation, back to the club, training again, fitness work, and finally home. His mother even learned to ride a TVS Scooty so she could help him get around with his equipment and food, sometimes following alongside as he cycled home.

Arshad's most memorable rule was 3,000 skips before training, every day. No skips, no training. His mother would sometimes sit and count them, knowing her son could lose count. Small actions created a big change. Shekhar began qualifying more consistently, his confidence returned, and his ranking climbed to around No. 136 in India in men's singles. The boy who had once considered quitting was moving forward again.

Talent Needs a Plan

When Arshad moved to Solapur, Shekhar found himself struggling once more, training hard but without a clear plan. "Results don't stay for long if there isn't a proper plan behind the work," he says. The next chapter came with coach Rupesh Roy, an experienced player who had once faced Novak Djokovic on the Under-18 circuit. Rupesh saw potential, but money was again a challenge, so Shekhar found a way: he trained for hours in the morning and taught younger children in the evening to make the arrangement possible. He wasn't simply paying for his dream. He was working for it.

At the same time he enrolled in computer science engineering at MIT in Aurangabad, while making clear that sport remained his primary goal. There were academic backlogs, and people questioned whether tennis was worth giving up a conventional career for. He came to accept that following your own path means not everyone will understand it. His mother kept standing behind him, and gradually the results spoke louder than the criticism. He qualified consistently, got seeded, and reached quarterfinals and semifinals. At one major tournament in Mumbai, he fought through qualifying to face a much higher-ranked opponent in the main draw.

What the Numbers Don't Show

Today, Shekhar is ranked No. 94 in India in singles and No. 54 in doubles. Those numbers don't show the railway stations, the two bags, the early mornings, the 3,000 skips, the three years of losing, or the nights he wondered whether to quit. They don't show the mother who kept believing when her son sometimes couldn't.

At seven, he picked up a racket out of curiosity. The most inspiring part of his journey is not where he stands today, but how many times he could have stopped, and chose not to.
