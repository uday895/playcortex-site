Shekhar Jaiswal

Shekhar Jaiswal and the mother who travelled to every tournament

"Whatever I am right now, it's because of my mom, because she never left me alone."

Shekhar Jaiswal found tennis by accident. He was seven, growing up in Aurangabad, now Chhatrapati Sambhaji Nagar, in Maharashtra, and one evening on his way to play cricket with friends he passed a tennis club by the roadside. He stopped to watch people hitting the ball and was amazed. His uncle, who was living with the family while he finished his studies, turned out to be friends with the coach, and soon Shekhar was playing there.

For the next six or seven years tennis was simply fun. He spent about three hours at the club, played with the other children and got some coaching. He had no idea there were ranking tournaments, a national association or points to earn.

An unranked boy at a state tournament

That changed in Class 10, around 2010 or 2011, when he represented his school at a state level DSO inter school tournament at Andheri Sports Club in Mumbai. He won one match 9 to 1, and the people watching began clapping. Someone asked him about his AITA ranking, and he had never heard of the All India Tennis Association. At the same tournament he beat a player ranked around 36 in India in the Under 18 category. Everyone was shocked, he remembers, that a boy with no ranking had beaten one of the top players.

His mother in particular decided he had real ability. After his board exams he moved from his first club to the MSLTA centre in Aurangabad, and after three months of training there he entered his first AITA event, an Under 18 tournament in Santa Cruz, Mumbai, in 2011. He walked on court with a small bag and one racket, and watched other players arrive fully kitted out. How they looked put more pressure on him than the tennis did. His mother told him to stick to the basics. "You have to play with the ball, not with your opponent. Do what you are trained for." He came through qualifying and lost in the second round of the main draw.

Two bags and a rice maker

From then on he was playing two tournaments a month all over India, and the costs added up: hotels, meals, travel, coaching fees, physio and fitness. "It's not easy for a middle-class person to pursue professional tennis," he says. His father is a bank manager on a fixed monthly salary, and his parents did not like asking anyone for help.

His mother travelled with him to every tournament until he was about 24. They carried two bags, one for clothes and one with a rice maker, a roti maker and ingredients, so they could cook in the hotel at night instead of spending around 1,000 rupees a day on food. Sometimes there was no money for a room at all. "Me and my mom, we have seen a lot of those days where we are just staying at the railway station, freshen up in the morning, and then going straight on the court to play the tournament," he says.

His father wanted him to concentrate on his studies and his marks. His mother backed the tennis. Relatives and neighbours asked why the family was wasting money, and some told his parents he would end up opening a shop and selling groceries. Through all of it she stayed beside him. "Whatever I am right now, it's because of my mom, because she never left me alone," he says. A dietitian was too expensive, so she asked the doctors and engineers on his father's side of the family to help plan his meals. He still avoids supplements and prefers natural food such as eggs.

He was one of many young players inspired by watching Sania Mirza on television, and like all of them he dreamed of reaching a Grand Slam one day. He also learned early that effort alone guarantees nothing. "If you are doing 100%, there are many players out there who are giving 200%," he says.

Three years of losing

When he turned 18 he moved into the men's category, where he could face players of any age. For three years he did not qualify for a single main draw. He would win one qualifying match and lose the next, and sometimes lose the first. He travelled from Aurangabad to Chandigarh, Sonipat and Surat, arriving on Thursday, signing in and practising on Friday, losing on Saturday and heading home on Sunday.

He began to think about quitting and spent long hours alone in his room trying to understand what was going wrong. He spoke to physios, a psychiatrist, his coach and his parents. He describes it as a kind of depression and believes most players go through a period like it. "This is a very lonely sport, because, you know, you are the alone person standing on the court right now, there is no one around you," he says.

3,000 skips a day

The turn came when a new coach, Arshad Warsi, arrived at the MSLTA centre. He was young and energetic, and he built Shekhar's training around the rest of his day. Shekhar was in Class 12 and preparing for IIT at Narayana. He woke at 3:30, was on court by 4:30 and trained until 7 or 7:30. He showered at the club and cycled to his coaching classes, which ran from 8 until 4, while his mother took his kit home. In the evening he cycled back to the club and she came again with his food on a TVS Scooty. She had never ridden even a bicycle before and learned for his sake. After fitness work they went home together, about six kilometres, racing each other, she on the scooter and he on his bicycle or sometimes on foot.

Arshad also took charge of his fitness and diet, and every day he spent half an hour talking with Shekhar about what was going on in his mind and what to do after missing a ball. He did not charge for any of it. His rule was simple. "If I'm coming to training, I have to do 3,000 skippings at home. If I do it, then only I go to training," Shekhar says. His mother sat next to him and counted, because it is easy to lose track. It took 15 to 20 minutes.

He started to qualify, earned a national ranking and, in the men's category, reached around 136 in India. He did not clear IIT, but with a rank of around 4,000 in Maharashtra he had choices, and he decided to stay in Aurangabad and study computer science engineering at MIT. The college agreed that sport was his main goal and that he would come in only for exams.

A coach who had played Djokovic

Early in his first year Arshad moved to Solapur, where he now runs his own academy, and Shekhar felt lost again. He was training hard but without a proper plan for which tournaments to play and how to prepare for them. "Yes, you see the results, but that won't stay longer if you don't have a perfect plan with you," he says.

Then Rupesh Roy came to Aurangabad as a personal coach for a client's child. Rupesh had been ranked around 400 in the world and, as an Under 18 player, had lost to Novak Djokovic 3 to 6, 4 to 6. After a trial he agreed to take Shekhar on. Other players paid him around 15,000 rupees a month. Shekhar paid 7,000 and made up the difference with work: five hours of training with Rupesh in the morning, then two hours teaching small children under him in the evening. It was the start of his own coaching, in his first year of engineering.

At a tournament in Panchgani with 2.5 lakh rupees in prize money, he qualified, won his first round and then lost a four hour match to the second seed, ranked around 46 or 47 in India, going down 3 to 7 in a third set tiebreak. Soon after, his first year results came out. He had failed four of his five subjects, with no attendance at all. The relatives and neighbours started again, and so did his father. Shekhar told himself he would stick to his plan even if he never became an engineer, and his mother argued with his father on his behalf.

Now a ranked player, he was seeded in qualifying draws, qualified regularly and began reaching quarterfinals and semifinals. His ranking rose to around 116. At the 12 lakh tournament in Santa Cruz, one of the biggest AITA events in the country, he won two qualifying rounds before losing in the third to a player ranked around 63 or 64. Then his second set of results arrived, and he now had nine subjects to clear before he could move into second year. Back from Mumbai, he sat down with his father and told him engineering was not possible for him.

Where he is now

Shekhar has been playing tennis for about 17 years and coaching for eight or nine of them. He is ranked 94 in India in men's singles and 54 in doubles.
