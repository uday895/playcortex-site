Manan Sood

One More Season

"People don't generally ask how you are doing mentally. They just see the results."

Manan Sood first held a bat at seven or eight, in his school's academy. A few months later the 2010 World Cup was on, he was watching Messi, and he switched to football. Cricket came back in sixth grade, when he was asked to captain his class in an inter-house match. He hit some good shots, and the school team coach happened to be watching.

Sitting Out, Then Standing Out

For a while he sat on the bench behind senior players, and it hurt. In one quiet practice session, Akash Sudan's father, his school coach at the time, threw balls at him and couldn't understand why he wasn't in the team. A few months later, at the Georgian Cup, Manan won Player of the Match in the first two games. "That was the point where I thought I could play cricket at a very good level."

After tenth grade he moved to Romesh Chopra's academy at the National Stadium. Eight or nine years later, he still trains there. He made his DDCA league debut in twelfth grade and took three wickets.

Nearly Quitting

In his first year of college, without an Under-16 or Under-19 cap, he was close to giving up. He decided to give it one more season and everything he had. He scored around 80 runs in his first four matches and then two hundreds and two fifties in the next four. The season after that, he was the third-highest run-scorer in Delhi's T20 league, with 409 runs in about ten matches.

That put his name into the DPL auction. He watched for eight hours with his friends; his mother followed on her phone upstairs. His name came up for about ten seconds, and nobody bought him. "At that moment I was completely broken."

The Blur

The next season, the doubt got into his batting. In one must-score T20 game he put so much pressure on himself that the ball looked blurry, and he was out for seven or eight. He started working with a sports psychologist, who introduced him to visualisation. These days he sleeps well before a match, visualises the game in the morning, and takes five to seven minutes alone before it starts.

Part of Life, Not All of It

He played through a shoulder injury in his best league season and did the rehab afterwards. He credits his parents and Romesh sir with talking him out of quitting more than once. Cricket, he has decided, is "a part of my life and not my life completely." He is also doing an internship in sports management. His goal is simple: play in the DPL. His advice to his younger self is to enjoy the game more.
