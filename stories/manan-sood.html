Manan Sood, the DPL Auction and One More Season

"This is a part of my life and I have to treat it as a part of my life and not my life completely."

When the first Delhi Premier League auction was held, Manan Sood sat in front of the television with his friends while his mother followed it on her phone upstairs. He had been the third highest run scorer in Delhi's T20 league that season. After eight hours of waiting, his name came up for about ten seconds, and nobody bought him. "At that moment I was completely broken," he says.

He had come close to giving up the game before, and he would come close again.

From football back to cricket

Manan first played cricket at seven or eight, when he joined the academy at his school. After four or five months his interest moved to football. The 2010 World Cup was on, he was fascinated by Messi, and for two or three years he played football at school.

Cricket came back by chance. In Class 6 he was asked to captain his class in an inter class match. He did not know the school team was picking players from those games. He hit some good shots, and the coach of the school team was watching. He was called to practice, and from then on cricket was his focus.

It was not straightforward. A few boys from Classes 7 and 8 made the school's playing eleven, but Manan was not one of them, because there were many senior players ahead of him. Sitting out hurt at times. His first trial, for the Under 14 team in Class 8, ended without selection, though he says that at that age he did not think of it as a low point.

He remembers one practice session after a semi final at the Brother Foley tournament at St. Columba's. Nobody else turned up, so his coach, Sudan sir, the father of Akash Sudan, spent the session throwing balls at him. Manan kept getting into good positions and hitting them well. The coach told him he could not understand why Manan was out of the team.

Two or three months later, at the Georgian Cup, he was named player of the match in the first game, with 30 or 35 runs and three wickets, and again in the second. His team lost the final. "I think that was the point where I thought I could play cricket at a very good level," he says.

One more season

The Under 16 season passed without anyone noticing him. After Class 10 he moved to Romesh Chopra's academy at the National Stadium, where he has now trained for eight or nine years. He made his DDCA league debut in Class 12 and took three wickets.

In his first year at college he began to think cricket was giving him nothing. He had not played Under 16 or Under 19 cricket, and he wondered whether he should stop. Instead he decided to give it one more season and everything he had. Before that he had scored only one or two hundreds. That season had eight matches. He made around 80 runs in the first four, then two hundreds and two fifties in the next four. "That was the first season that I thought yes I am really good at it and I should keep doing it," he says.

The following season, in 2023 or 2024, he scored 409 runs in about ten matches in the T20 league, with five or six fifties and a highest score of 89 or 90. He was the third highest run scorer in Delhi, which he counts as his biggest achievement because he had not expected it of himself. He played that season through a shoulder injury, deciding to keep going rather than dwell on it, and did his rehab afterwards so that he would be able to throw again.

After the auction

Those runs brought his name into the DPL auction. He felt he had done enough, and had performed well in the trials too, so going unsold was the biggest setback of his career. He says he is still getting over it.

Again he thought about leaving cricket, and again he chose to try one more season. This time his mind would not settle. Doubts about whether he would ever play in the DPL kept running through his head. He made 500 runs in the 40 over league, but his T20 season fell short. In one T20 game in 2025, with the auction approaching, he was told that every match counted and that runs would give him a chance. He put so much pressure on himself that he could not see the ball properly. "Everything was so blur," he says. He was out for seven or eight.

The months up to February were the hardest of his career. He could not score runs or get into position, and he struggled to sleep. He began working with a sports psychologist, who introduced him to visualisation, and he says it has helped him concentrate on the ball and get into a better frame of mind.

How he prepares now

Before a match he tries to calm his thoughts and get seven or eight hours of sleep. On match mornings he is up around six. He bathes, does a little puja, visualises how he will play, and meditates. At the ground he takes five to seven minutes alone in a corner to go over what he needs to do for the conditions, then goes out to play. He works with a strength and conditioning coach who helps him manage his workload, and he used to work with a nutritionist. He is also doing an internship in sports management, fitting in strength and power training five days a week, practice four days and a match on the other.

He credits his parents and Romesh sir with keeping him in the game. There were times he thought about quitting, and his parents told him it is important to fight. When he talked to his coach about leaving, Romesh sir told him that things can change in a day or a month and that what matters is to keep grinding.

When he did imagine stepping away, it did not help. If he is not on the ground or in the gym, the day feels strange. "This is a part of my life and I have to treat it as a part of my life and not my life completely," he says. What he loves is the moment of facing a fast bowler in morning conditions with no thoughts in his head. He is trying to let go of thinking about playing for the state or the country and to enjoy his game.

That is also what he would tell his younger self. He was too caught up in his own performances and did not enjoy them. He believes he has the potential to play at a higher level. His goal for next season is simple: to play in the DPL.
