Arunima

Arunima, from her first chess set to Miranda House

"Making it to nationals in my very first year of playing felt like my real breakthrough into competitive chess."

Arunima began chess in third grade because a close friend mentioned she was taking lessons. It was the first time Arunima had proper coaching and the first time she owned a chess set. The early memories are small ones: afternoon and evening classes with a coach who came to her home, and rewards like a pen or a pizza party when she and her friend did well. "Those early memories, getting my first chess set, my first chess clock, are what stand out the most," she says.

She credits that first coach with shaping how she still feels about the game. He treated chess as something to enjoy rather than a rigid, high pressure pursuit.

Playing older students

In fourth grade her coach thought she was ready to join the after school sessions, which meant playing middle and senior school students several years older. Her parents were uneasy about a fourth grader taking on pupils from seventh, eighth and ninth grade, but she gradually began to win, and that was the first sign to all of them that this was more than a passing interest.

The same year she tried out for her school's zonal team without much expectation, having played for barely a year. She and the friend who had introduced her to the game were both picked. The team finished fourth, enough to qualify for nationals. "Making it to nationals in my very first year of playing felt like my real breakthrough into competitive chess," she says. Getting that far so quickly convinced her she could go further.

Favouritism and Covid

The calm of those early years did not last. She was improving steadily and building a rating when Covid moved everything online. She kept playing tournaments virtually, but it was not the same, and she lost time and touch with the game. In sixth and seventh grade she also ran into favouritism within her training group. It happened in two or three separate phases, and each was discouraging enough that she told her parents she wanted to quit. She kept playing for herself and in eleventh grade won a national level position, but after that year she stopped playing competitively for her school. "It really does take a toll on your mental health," she says.

Rebuilding a steady routine has been a struggle ever since, made harder now by the awkward gaps between early morning chess practice and college classes.

Handling losses

Losing streaks and stalled ratings once sent her into a difficult place over even half a point. She now reminds herself that top players go through the same swings and that one loss does not settle anything. Music, time with chess friends spent talking about something other than chess, or simply sitting alone help her reset before the next tournament. "I know I've faced setbacks before and come back from them," she says, "and that's made me more adaptable, both to people and to the sport itself."

Her preparation for a tournament builds slowly. A month out she plays a game or two online each day. Closer to the date she switches to puzzles to sharpen how she reads the board, and if she knows her opponent she studies their games to prepare openings and cover her own weak points. About two days before, she eases off and skips outings and plans. "What works for a top player doesn't automatically work for everyone," she says.

Chess and economics

She now studies economics honours at Miranda House and sees a direct link between the two. Opportunity cost, giving something up to gain something else, appears on the board all the time: a plan that costs a piece but improves the position, or one that wins a piece and leaves you worse placed. Game theory maps onto chess in a similar way, and she says that kind of thinking now carries into her daily life.

Given unlimited resources, she would invest in players aged eleven to thirteen who have the ability but are held back by the cost of travel, accommodation and entry fees, since most major events are held abroad. She would also like more international tournaments hosted in India. For herself, she would choose a week long camp with the grandmasters she admires, Divya Deshmukh among them.

What kept her playing for her school through all of it was the team events. She travelled to nationals with the same friend three times, and each trip felt like a small holiday. Over the next two years she wants to help her college win the intercollege tournament, return to informal coaching and keep playing for as long as she can. In five words she calls herself calculated, focused, adaptable, dedicated and composed.
