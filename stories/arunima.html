Arunima

The Board Behind the Books

"Making it to nationals in my very first year of playing felt like my real breakthrough."

Arunima's chess journey started almost by accident. In third grade, a close friend mentioned she was taking coaching, and Arunima decided to try it too. That was the first time she got proper tutoring, and the first time she owned a chess set of her own. The early days were mostly about the small things: afternoon or evening classes, a coach who came home to teach her, little rewards like a pen or a pizza party whenever she and her friend did well. "Those early memories, getting my first chess set, my first chess clock, are what stand out the most," she says.

Playing Up

By fourth grade, her coach saw enough potential to suggest she join the after-school stay-backs, putting her up against middle and senior school students years older than her. Her parents were apprehensive about a fourth grader competing against seventh, eighth, and ninth graders, but she started winning gradually, and that was the first sign, to her and to them, that this might be more than a passing interest.

The real turning point came that same year. She gave trials for her school's zonal team competition without much expectation, having played for barely a year. She and the friend who'd first got her into the game were both selected, and their team finished fourth, enough to qualify for nationals. "Making it to nationals in my very first year of playing felt like my real breakthrough into competitive chess," she says. The fact that she'd gotten that far within a year convinced her she could go much further if she kept at it.

A Fun Game, Made Difficult

She credits her first coach with shaping how she still feels about the sport today, someone who treated chess as something enjoyable rather than the rigid, high-pressure activity it's often made out to be. That calm foundation would matter later, because the environment around her didn't stay that way. Through sixth and seventh grade, even as she kept improving and building a rating, she ran into favouritism within her training group. It happened across two or three separate phases, each demotivating enough that she told her parents she wanted to quit. She didn't. She kept playing for herself, eventually winning a national-level position in eleventh grade, but after that year she stopped playing competitively for her school. "It really does take a toll on your mental health," she says.

COVID and the Long Way Back

COVID had already interrupted her rhythm. She'd been steadily improving, gaining a rating and enjoying the process, when everything moved online. She tried to keep playing tournaments virtually, but it wasn't the same, and she lost real time and touch with the game. Rebuilding a consistent routine since then has remained one of her ongoing struggles, made harder now by the odd gaps between early morning chess practice and college classes.

Losing Without Losing Herself

Where losing streaks and stalled rankings once sent her into a difficult headspace over even half a point, she's since built a steadier relationship with setbacks. She reminds herself that other top players face the same swings, and that one loss doesn't define anything permanently. Music, time with chess friends spent not talking about chess, or simply sitting alone, all help her reset before the next tournament. "I know I've faced setbacks before and come back from them," she says, "and that's made me more adaptable, both to people and to the sport itself."

Chess, Economics, and the Same Kind of Thinking

Now studying Economics Honours at Miranda House, Arunima sees a direct line between the two disciplines. The idea of opportunity cost, giving something up to gain something else, shows up constantly on the board: choosing between a plan that costs a piece but improves position, or one that gains a piece but leaves you worse placed. Concepts like game theory map onto chess in much the same way. It's the kind of trade-off thinking she says now carries into her daily life.

The Preparation Behind the Calm

Her pre-tournament routine builds gradually. A month out, she starts playing a match or two daily online; as the date nears, she shifts to solving puzzles to sharpen her ability to read the board, and if she knows her opponent, she researches their games to prepare targeted openings and shore up her own weak points. About two days before, she pulls back a little, stepping away from outings and plans, easing into focus rather than forcing it. "What works for a top player doesn't automatically work for everyone," she says.

What She'd Change

Asked where she'd invest with unlimited resources, her answer is pointed: toward young players, eleven to thirteen years old, who are capable but held back by the cost of travel, boarding, and tournament fees, since most major events happen abroad. She'd also like to see more global tournaments hosted in India itself. For herself, she'd want a week-long camp with the grandmasters she admires, Divya Deshmukh among them.

Community, More Than Competition

What kept her playing for her school through all of it wasn't just the results. It was the team tournaments, travelling to nationals with the same friend three times, each trip feeling like a small vacation. It's a community that doesn't need constant contact to still feel real. In the next two years, she'd like to help her college win the intercollege tournament, get back into informal coaching, and keep playing for as long as she can. In five words she is calculated, focused, adaptable, dedicated, and composed, a description built one difficult year at a time.
