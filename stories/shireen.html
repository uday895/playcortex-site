Shireen

Shireen, the 400 metre runner who keeps turning up

"Isn't it better if a few years down the line I get to say despite all this, I still did it?"

At nineteen, Shireen is often introduced as "she's an athlete" before anyone says her name. She runs the 400 metres, trains at the Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium in Delhi and is a second year Psychology Honours student at SD College. "Now it's such a big part of my identity that I can't imagine my life without it."

Shoes, not spikes

Her grandfather, her Nanu, was a sports journalist. From the time she was five he told her she had the build of a runner, with broad shoulders and strong legs. Her father wanted her to be active and had her try tennis, basketball, horse riding and football, and she gave up each of them. "The more they tell you to do something, the more you don't want to." Athletics came almost by chance, through training run by a PE teacher at school.

At an Under 14 inter school race she ran in ordinary running shoes while the other girls wore spikes, and she won gold. "Wait, maybe I'm actually good at this."

The Covid lockdown in 2020 made her serious. Sports grounds were among the few places still open, so she trained every morning and every evening. "It kind of slowly became my identity that I'm an athlete, I'm fast." Her coach in Chandigarh, Maninder Heerasar, saw her potential and persuaded her father to back a professional career. She still connects the cloudy weather of October and November with how much she loves training.

Fourth, and then gold

In 2022 she finished fourth at the Youth Nationals in Bhopal. It was not a podium, but it brought calls from coaches across India. Sprinting in India had long been dominated by athletes from the southern states, and here was a girl from Chandigarh. Almost overnight she went from unknown to prospect.

She has represented India three times: at a junior Asian event in South Korea, at the Youth Commonwealth Games in Trinidad and Tobago, and at the Youth Asian Championship in Tashkent, Uzbekistan. In Tashkent in 2023 she won gold in the relay. Her best memory is the victory lap with the national flag and standing on the podium while the national anthem played. Her school, Strawberry Fields in Chandigarh, helped her keep up with her syllabus and projects while she competed abroad in twelfth grade. While many people her age were out partying, she was up for training at five in the morning, and she is grateful for it.

Fourth place kept coming back, though: twice at Youth Nationals and in the individual 400 metres in Asia. "I feel like if I run alone also, I'll come fourth," she says.

Injury and a difficult year

A grade two adductor and groin injury kept her out for seven to eight months. The physical recovery was only part of it; she also had to get past the fear that the pain would return. She found the right medical support in Delhi.

In 2024 she found herself in a toxic coaching environment, and a slump in form brought public criticism. For a while the track stopped feeling like a comfortable place. There were days she called her mother to say she wanted to quit, but she never stopped going to training. She decided she would rather be able to look back one day and say she had done it in spite of everything. "Isn't it better if a few years down the line I get to say despite all this, I still did it?"

Eighty per cent mental

Before races she used to feel weak and dizzy, and she put it down to her diet or the weather. Her psychology studies helped her recognise it as anxiety. In the 400 metres she now talks herself round the whole lap. "I'm talking to myself continuously." For her the sport is 80 per cent mental, and she sees sports psychology as an essential field. The thing she is proudest of is not a medal but turning up to training every day, whatever her mood and whatever else is going on in her life.

Her mother, whom she calls her "momager", stays with her at every national competition and is patient through her mood swings. "She's my pillar."

No Indian woman has won an Olympic medal in her event, and that is the goal she has set herself. "I still have that privilege that I can achieve that."
