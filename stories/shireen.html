Shireen

Despite All This, I Still Did It

"I still have that privilege that I can achieve that."

At nineteen, Shireen is often introduced as an athlete before anyone says her name. She trains at the Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium in Delhi and studies Psychology Honours at SD College. Her track story has less to do with medals than with stubbornly turning up.

A Grandfather's Prophecy

From the time she was five, her Nanu, a sports journalist, told her she had a runner's build: broad shoulders and strong legs. Her father had her try tennis, basketball, horse riding and football, and she gave up every one of them. She found athletics almost by accident, through a school PE teacher's training session.

At an Under-14 inter-school race she ran in regular shoes while the others wore spikes, and she won gold. "Wait, maybe I'm actually good at this."

From Unknown to Prospect

During the 2020 lockdown, while the rest of the world slowed down, the grounds stayed open. She trained every morning and every evening, and running became part of who she was. "It kind of slowly became my identity that I'm an athlete; I'm fast."

In 2022 she finished fourth at the Youth Nationals in Bhopal. A girl from Chandigarh in a sport long dominated by southern states, she suddenly had coaches from across India calling. She went on to represent India three times: at the Junior Asian meet in South Korea, the Youth Commonwealth Games in Trinidad and Tobago, and the Youth Asian Championship in Tashkent. In Tashkent she won relay gold. What stayed with her most was not the medal but the national anthem.

Fourth, Again

Fourth place kept coming back: twice at Youth Nationals and in the individual 400m in Asia. "I feel like if I run alone also, I'll come fourth," she once joked. In January 2023 a grade-two adductor and groin injury kept her out for seven to eight months, until she found the right medical support in Delhi.

In 2024 a toxic coaching environment and public criticism after a slump left her drained. There were days she phoned her mother to say she wanted to quit, but she never stopped going to training.

Eighty Percent Mental

Her psychology studies helped her see that the dizziness before races was anxiety, not diet or weather. Now she talks to herself all the way through the 400m. For her, the sport is 80% mental. Her mother, her "momager," has been her anchor throughout.

No Indian woman has won an Olympic medal in her event, and she wants to be the first. She would rather be able to say she got there in spite of everything: "Despite all this, I still did it."
