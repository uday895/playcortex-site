Sidharth Kumar

Learning to Manage the Mind

"Nothing is guaranteed in cricket, especially at the senior level."

Sidharth Kumar grew up around cricket. His father played Ranji Trophy cricket. Sidharth started at nine at Frank Anthony School because he wanted to, and early on he decided he would become a professional fast bowler.

Self-Driven, Early On

He reached the Under-14 Nationals mostly on his own discipline. Before he joined Akash Sudan's setup, he felt coaches had already decided what kind of player he was, which hurt his confidence.

He went on to represent Delhi at Under-16 and Under-19. The Under-16 season was the hardest. He felt he had been treated unfairly in selection, then picked up a strain and was dropped for two matches. Against Bihar he chose to sit out rather than play through the pain, a decision he still regrets. A poor quarterfinal followed, and he entered what he calls a long "bad phase."

A Second Injury

In his second Under-19 year, a severe strain stopped him again. Setbacks and missed expectations led to self-doubt, and the negative voice in his head began to affect how he played.

In December 2023 he met Gavnish bhaiya, who became a friend and mentor. Gavnish and Akash Sudan taught him to separate what he could control (A factors) from what he couldn't (B factors). When the controllables are handled well, the rest matters less.

Separating Self From Sport

He began to see the difference between his conscious mind, which was busy, negative and fixated on detail, and the quieter state where he played freely. Learning to switch between them became daily work. The biggest change came when he stopped treating cricket as his whole identity. That brought him some peace.

Even after nearly two years without playing, he was picked for the Delhi Under-19 one-day side in 2025. He did well under pressure on debut. He struggled against Punjab in the next game, but it no longer shook him. Sagar bhaiya told him to drop bad days quickly and move on like a professional, and head coach Raju Sharma backed him. He went on to have a strong season.

Fighter, Funny, Flamboyant

As he moves into senior cricket, he sees the game as one part of a bigger life. He credits his father for always treating failure as normal, and the Sudan family for their support. He remembers calling Akash bhaiya after a bad Under-14 match in the Cooch Behar tournament and calming down just by talking to him.

Fighter, funny, flamboyant: that is how he describes himself. Learning to handle bad days, protect his headspace and enjoy the process is now central to how he plays.
