Sourabh

Sourabh, the Hindu College goalkeeper the crowd called Sandy

"I can't live without football."

When the penalty shootout began, half the stadium was chanting his nickname. "Whole stadium was chanting Sandy, Sandy, Sandy," Sourabh remembers. "For me it's like a pressure." He saved a penalty that day and Hindu College kept its intercollege football title. He is 22, a goalkeeper from a middle class Delhi family with roots in Bihar, and the first person in his family to take up sport.

Street football and a no from home

Like many children in Delhi, he started with evening games in a nearby park after school and tuition. He was in the sixth standard, and he played without fear, so older boys told him he should play properly for a team. At his government school, where he says the team existed mostly on paper, he went for his first trial in 2016 and 2017. That year the school won a sub junior tournament, beating the area's strongest side in the semi final.

After Class 10 came the choice of stream. Sourabh told his parents he wanted to be a footballer. His father said no: football was a game for fun, not a career, and he should take science. For three or four days his father did not speak to him. His mother backed him. In the break after his board exams he went to the government academy at the Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium with a friend, found his way to the admissions office in the heat, filled in the forms and enrolled himself.

No fear at the stadium

The academy was packed, with four or five hundred players a day across six or seven batches, training from 11 in the morning until eight or nine at night. His first coach there, Harish, noticed that the new goalkeeper had no fear. Sourabh, then 16, trained with senior players in their twenties, some of whom played in top leagues or represented the Air Force and Navy. He travelled with the senior team to practice matches, mostly on the bench, and learned from all of it. Harish held up a goalkeeper called Mola as the example to follow.

On Harish's advice he moved to a school where football was a priority. To get in he had to repeat Class 11, and for five or six months he trained alone on an empty ground in the afternoons, carrying a kit bag of 14 or 15 kilograms, preparing for the School Games Federation of India trials. In 2020, after a long selection process, he was picked for Delhi in the Under 17 age group. "That was literally my turning point," he says. His father was happy for him at last.

At the nationals, Delhi lost in the pre quarter finals, and before anything more could come of it the pandemic stopped play. A teacher at his new school, which was semi private, knew his family could not easily afford the fees. He spoke to the principal and arranged a full scholarship. "He did so much for me," Sourabh says. In 2022, in Class 12, his school won the Delhi state school championship without conceding a goal.

From Bhagat Singh to Hindu College

When college places came out, his friends got into the colleges they wanted. Sourabh got Shaheed Bhagat Singh College, and he cried in front of his parents. His father told him not to worry. The first year turned out well. Bhagat Singh was an underrated team that won several tournaments, and he trained under Ashish, a senior goalkeeper who he says has since signed for Mumbai City.

Then a teammate rang with news. Hindu College needed a goalkeeper and could take him through migration after his first year. Hindu was his dream college, and his father's too. His friends told him to go. Many others, who expected him to become Bhagat Singh's first choice keeper, called to tell him to stay.

He accepted. The last day of the migration portal fell on a Saturday, and the college office was closed. A friend told him to go straight to the principal, who was known to be strict. When the principal saw the Hindu College letterhead and heard that Sourabh was a goalkeeper, he agreed to sign. It was about 3.30 in the afternoon, and Sourabh ran back to the metro station.

At Hindu, where he studied Political Science Honours, he joined a class that included students with 800 out of 800, and some classmates assumed he must be the child of someone important. He remains grateful to the staff who helped him through the move, especially the teacher students knew as Hansu sir, who treated him like a friend.

The shootout

The 2023 intercollege season was the first since the pandemic. Hindu had only four sports quota players; the rest of the squad had come in through academics. They drew with Hansraj, beat SRCC 1 to 0 and conceded early against IGIPESS before recovering to reach the semi finals. The decisive match was against Venkateswara College, one of the strongest sides, where Mola, the keeper Sourabh had once been told to copy, was now playing. It went to penalties, in front of a stadium split between the two colleges.

After a teammate missed, one of Sourabh's saves was ruled out because the referee said he had come off his line, and the kick was retaken. He spoke to the taker, telling him which way he would dive, trying to put doubt in his mind. He saved it. Hindu College had defended its championship, and some of the people who had told him not to leave Bhagat Singh now called to say he had made the right decision.

In the inter university championship that followed, he saved penalties again, including one that decided whether the team went home, and Delhi University qualified for the All India stage for the first time in 18 years. "2023 is like a very magical year for me," he says.

Injury and what comes next

In January 2026, after qualifying for the All India stage again, he injured his ACL in Rajasthan and had surgery at a sports injury centre. In July he was walking and jogging lightly but not yet running. The specialists there now guide his diet. He first changed what he ate years ago, when a coach told him before his first national tournament to start eating chicken and eggs. He had never used whey protein or creatine and still scored 100 percent in fitness tests, but he has started taking supplements during his recovery.

He knows how many young players train without proper meals. At the stadium he often had no lunch, going straight from school to training and eating next at dinner. He thinks parents, schools and academies need to make sure young players get proper food and water, and he says psychological support matters as much as nutrition. The injury has been hard on him. While his batchmates keep playing, he sometimes goes back over his last year at Hindu and feels the team lost because of him.

His degree is now finished. He wants to do a postgraduate course at a university where he can keep playing, and after his playing days he wants to stay in the game, as a coach, in sports management or with an academy of his own. "I can't live without football," he says. A school friend once told him, "Sandy, you are nothing to everything," and he has kept that line with him.
