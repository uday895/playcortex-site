Sandeepti Singh Rao

Sandeepti Singh Rao and the knee injury that tested her love of tennis

"My comeback, in many ways, belongs to them as much as it belongs to me."

Sandeepti Singh Rao was a hyperactive child who found it hard to sit still, so her mother looked for somewhere to put that energy and enrolled her at a tennis academy nearby. Nobody was trying to make a professional of her. "At that stage, tennis was not a dream, a goal, or a future; it was just something I enjoyed doing."

For a while it stayed that way. She loved being on court, hitting the ball and running around. The first real shift came when she entered a district level tournament and won it. To her parents, the win showed that she was competitive, that she could handle pressure and that her interest in the game was genuine. They decided to support her seriously and began looking for a good academy where she could train properly.

Injured at her best

Over the years tennis moved to the centre of her life. Training became more intense, expectations grew, and she reached one of the strongest phases of her career. It was then, while she was playing well and feeling confident, that she suffered a major knee injury that needed surgery.

She was not prepared for what it did to her mind. The same questions played on repeat: whether she would compete again, whether she would ever play at the same level or win matches again, whether her body would feel the same after surgery, and what would happen if she came back and got injured again. "These thoughts were exhausting and emotionally draining, and they often felt heavier than the physical pain itself."

She cried a lot. There were days of frustration, anger and helplessness, and at her lowest she thought about leaving tennis altogether, because giving up felt easier than living with the uncertainty and the pain. Her parents kept believing in her when she had lost belief in herself. "My comeback, in many ways, belongs to them as much as it belongs to me."

A stick and a knee brace

The restrictions lasted a long time. Until her knee had fully recovered she could not do ordinary adventurous things like ice skating or trampolining. She went to college with a stick and a knee brace, which she found hard and which made her feel different from everyone around her. Having to be careful with her leg all the time made her anxious, and she worried about her body and her future more than she likes to admit.

Rehabilitation was long and painful, and on some days she hated every minute of it. She kept turning up even when her motivation had gone, "because I believed that giving up would hurt more than trying." She has always thought of herself as someone with a strong mindset, and this was the period that tested it. Her parents were beside her again, through every setback and every small win.

Where she stands now

Her relationship with tennis is not simple any more. She calls it love and hate at times, with frustration, doubt and exhaustion sitting alongside a deep attachment. "Tennis is something I truly cannot live without." She credits it with making her strong and giving her an identity.

The injury also left her with a view about sport in India. She believes many people do not understand the mental side of being an athlete, and that injuries, setbacks and pressure affect an athlete's confidence and emotional wellbeing as deeply as they affect the body. Whatever happens next, she says, tennis will always mean more to her than wins or losses.
