Kashish Pansari, the Footballer Who Started Out Scared of the Ball

"Playing the sport keeps the inner child inside me alive."

Kashish Pansari has played football in goal, in midfield and in defence, at district and zonal level in Mumbai and for Lady Shri Ram College in Delhi. When she started, she was frightened of the ball. "I was very scared of the ball hitting my face," she says.

She had moved to Mumbai in fourth standard and was new at school, trying to fit in. Every student had to pick an activity after school hours, and "all the cool kids were going for football," so she picked it too. Two of her teammates decided to cure her fear. One held her against a wall while the other kept throwing the ball at her face, on purpose. "Yes, yeah, it's a real story," she says. It was horrifying at the time, but she now thinks "that was very much needed." The fear went, and she began to love the game.

So close to state level

She played inter school competitions, then district level, then zonals. Her team qualified for the next round, which could have meant representing Maharashtra at state level. Then the Diwali holidays came. Teammates were out of town, and although parents tried to persuade other families to send their children, the team could not travel. "It was a very sad moment."

Her school coach later started his own academy nearby, Kopana Football School, and she joined. She was the first girl there, and for a long time she trained only with boys. Gradually her friends joined, a separate girls' team was formed, and the academy has grown a great deal since.

Every position on the pitch

For years she played in goal. The team did not have a keeper and she wanted a place in the starting eleven, so she stepped up. Then a Mumbai newspaper printed her photograph and mentioned her by name for her goalkeeping. "That's when it grew on me," she says. She liked the role precisely because it is rarely celebrated: people talk about whoever scored the goal, not the keeper. When her school needed a handball team, she went in goal there too, and was praised for it.

When she changed schools in eighth standard, the trials were already over and the team had a goalkeeper. She went to the coach on her own, asked for an individual trial, and was placed as a defensive midfielder. She played there steadily until tenth standard. As a junior in the Under 17 and Under 18 side she felt intimidated by the older players, but adapted. In eleventh and twelfth, many of her teammates moved to the IB section and the team split, leaving her, as she puts it, "low-key, the only person remaining." Rebuilding the side was hard, and her own game dipped for a while.

Some moments stayed with her. A refereeing decision in ninth standard that she felt was biased took an emotional toll. She received one yellow card in school, her first and last, and it left her shaken.

Lady Shri Ram College

At Lady Shri Ram College the football team was still new, started by a senior. In her first year there were very few players. More arrived in her second year, and by her third there was a proper team. They recently finished fourth at the Inter DU tournament. She plays in defence now. "I have a soft spot for goalkeeping," she says, though she feels a little out of touch with it.

She has played through a minor ligament tear against her physio's advice, and her knees hurt if she runs too fast. "It happens to my mom also, so I think that's genetic or whatever." In one recent match an opponent scratched her during play. "I started calling the ref like bro," she says. They lost that match. She sees pressure as useful when a team is well bonded, and uses it herself to give her best.

Music, and a weekend club

In sixth standard, like many of her friends, she was obsessed with Messi and wanted to play professionally. That ambition faded, not through self doubt but because she has always had many interests. She does not even enjoy watching much sport.

She is in her final semester now. She has released her first song and is working towards a career as an independent musician. Football is staying, though. After college she plans to join a weekend club, not to compete seriously but to keep playing. "Playing the sport keeps the inner child inside me alive," she says.

Her younger brother has now taken up football with the same enthusiasm she once had. Watching him play, she says, has been a "full circle moment."
