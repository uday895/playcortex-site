Pranoy Borah

Pranoy Borah, the dropped catch and the way back to cricket

"In cricket it's just one good game to find your mojo."

Pranoy Borah still remembers the catch. Keeping wicket for a district side in Assam when he was around 16, he dropped a crucial chance. The batter went on to make 70, and his team lost and failed to reach the next stage. "I still have that vision of me dropping that catch," he says. It led him to walk away from academy cricket. Years later he found his way back to the game, and at 21 he coaches children and is preparing for a career in the business of cricket.

Gully cricket and the academy

He started at six, playing in the living room. He was born and brought up in Ahmedabad, where cricket meant gully games in his housing society in the evenings and playing with his father at weekends. His parents are from Assam. The real start came in 2015, when the family moved to Delhi and he joined the RPCA academy, aged nine or ten. His first coach, Mahi Goswami, taught him how to hold a bat, and Pranoy still works under him today.

Within six months he moved from a soft ball to the leather ball, and then up through the academy's groups. Each step was more serious than the last, with more nets, more drills and longer hours. Sunday was match day, and he would be up at six to get there. In the senior group, at 11 or 12, he was keeping wicket to much bigger boys and broke his thumb. "I was so scared of the ball," he says. He kept going, and went on outstation tours to Dabasi, Jaipur and Dubai. The trip that mattered most was to Assam, where he did well at trials and played for a district team.

The catch

He had travelled from Delhi, and the team relied on him behind the stumps. When the catch went down and the match was lost, he says, "it was a crush of emotions". He cried a great deal. During another important league soon afterwards, he left in the middle and went home to Delhi. "It was just too much." His mother comforted him. His father was upset at the way he had come back and felt he should have been stronger.

Looking back, he does not think the catch itself was the real setback. "The biggest setback would be that I did not have the headstrong mentality and the maturity to keep continuing," he says. A 13 year old who fails thinks it will never work out for him, he adds, "but actually you never know what's going to happen when you turn 16, when you turn 18."

By 2021 he had stopped playing for the academy. COVID followed, then Classes 11 and 12, and for about two years he hardly played. He spent much of that time gaming with three friends, sometimes ten hours a day, and got a dog, who gave him something new to pour his love into. He does not regret those years.

Falling back in love

In 2023 a friend at Hindu College asked if he wanted to try out for the college team. He played for Hindu for three years and found that the game had changed for him once he stopped measuring himself against playing for India. "When I stopped thinking in those lines, my perspective of the game completely changed," he says. Sessions ran from noon until four, and everyone stayed on until six, eating in the canteen and talking. Hindu beat St Stephen's three years running, but lost a semi final to PGDAV and intercollege quarter finals to Satyawati and Ambedkar.

His parents, he says, have been the most supportive people throughout. He got the equipment and training he asked for, no questions asked, and his father never pressed him about academics. "He always just wanted to see me play," Pranoy says. His closest friends were the boys he trained with for hours every day. "We breathe cricket," he says.

Coaching under 13s

Since June 2024 he has coached children under 13 at an academy. It keeps him close to the game without the winning and losing that weighed on him as a player. He teaches fundamentals, and also the mental side that was missing from his own training. After a match he splits his talk with a child in half, the first part on what went wrong and the second on what went well. And he tells them that the day ends when they leave the ground. "As soon as you walk out of the ground, this chapter of your day closes," he says. In cricket, he reminds them, "it's just one good game to find your mojo."

A 12 year old may not need a conventional psychologist, he thinks, but he does need someone to talk to, and Pranoy wants to be that person. He believes making it big should be half of what drives a young player. "The rest 50% should be the love, because in the end, if you're not passionate and loving about something, you'll never make it anyways." His simplest advice is to ask your six year old self why you started playing.

Fitness, home food and the UK

For Pranoy, fitness is the main element of cricket. "You have the technique, you have the talent to play 100 balls, but you do not have the ability to stand in the heat for 100 balls, then you're never getting 100," he says. As a boy he was told to face 150 balls in the nets, with runs not mattering. After school, lunch and academy sessions that ran until about 7.30, he would run for 45 minutes around his colony, listening to music. Sundays brought an hour of what he calls brutal fitness work: planks, situps and push ups. When his knees began to trouble him, a doctor told him to stop running on the road and he moved to a treadmill. His food was his mother's home cooking of rice, dal, chicken and vegetables, with no cold drinks or junk food, and he thinks that is enough for a 13 or 14 year old.

Having finished his degree at Hindu College, he was due to start a master's in sports management in the UK in September 2026, his first time living away from home. He wants to enjoy what he studies, which he did not in his bachelor's. In the long run he wants to work for a franchise team, whether in the IPL, the Big Bash, the Caribbean Premier League, The Hundred or another cricket league.
