Pranoy Borah

Remember why you started playing

"Go back and ask yourself the six-year-old version of yourself, why did you start playing."

Pranoy Borah's cricket story began in his living room at six and nearly ended with a single dropped catch at 16. What makes it worth telling is what came after: a return to the game for pure joy, a growing passion for coaching young children, and a clear plan to build a career in the business of cricket.

Living room, gully and academy

Based in Delhi, with family roots in Assam, Pranoy started the way so many Indian kids do. He played in the living room, then gully cricket in his society in the evenings, and with his father on weekends. His formal training began in 2015 at an academy in Delhi, where he moved from soft ball to hard ball and up through the age groups, with nets and matches every Sunday.

The senior group brought the first real challenges. He broke a finger keeping wicket at 11 or 12 against much bigger boys and admits he was scared of the ball. He kept trying anyway. He went on outstation tours, including to Jaipur and Dubai, and travelled to Assam for trials, where he played district cricket by 16.

The catch

The defining moment came in a district match in Assam. Pranoy dropped a crucial catch, the batsman went on to make 70, and his team lost and failed to qualify for the next stage. It was, he says, a crush of emotions. Soon after, in the middle of another important league, he simply left and came home to Delhi. It was too much. His family felt he should have been stronger, and the period was difficult. Looking back, he says his biggest setback was not the catch but not yet having the headstrong mentality and maturity to keep going. A 13-year-old who fails thinks it will never work out, he says, but you never know what will happen when you turn 16 or 18.

After COVID and the pressure of Classes 11 and 12, he stopped playing completely for a couple of years.

Falling back in love

In 2023, at Hindu College, he rediscovered the game. For three years he played for the college team with a completely different perspective. When he stopped thinking about playing for India, the game stopped being a burden. Sessions became long afternoons of cricket, food in the canteen and friendship. Hindu College beat strong sides along the way, though the team often fell short in knockout stages.

His parents, he says, have been the most supportive people throughout, providing whatever training and equipment he needed without question. Even when he quit, his father just wanted to see him play. His friends were the boys he trained with for hours every day, and they grew up breathing cricket together.

Coaching the next generation

Since June 2024, Pranoy has been coaching children under 13, and he loves it. He teaches fundamentals, but also the mental side that was missing in his own training. After a match, he spends half the conversation on mistakes and half on what the child did well. He tells young players that a bad game ends when they step off the ground, and that in cricket it only takes one good game to find your mojo again. Young kids may not need a formal psychologist, he believes, but they do need someone to talk to.

He believes passion should be at least half of what drives an athlete, with the ambition to make it big as the other half. If you are not passionate about something, he says, you will never make it anyway.

Endurance, home food and the road ahead

Pranoy calls fitness the most important part of the game. Technique is useless if you cannot stand in the heat for 100 balls. As a boy he was told to face 150 balls in the nets, runs not mattering, and he built endurance with a daily 45-minute run around his colony and brutal Sunday fitness sessions. Strength training, he says, does not have to mean a gym. Nutrition for young kids can simply be good home food, as his mother made sure of with rice, dal, chicken and vegetables.

Outside cricket he is fun-loving, loves his dog and spent much of the COVID years gaming. Now he is heading to the UK for a master's in sports management. His long-term goal is to work for a professional franchise league team, whether in the IPL, the Big Bash or the Caribbean Premier League. Whatever happens, he will keep asking his six-year-old self why he started.
