Kanishka Kumawat

Kanishka Kumawat had to call her father Sir at the ground

"That was the moment I stopped relying on him as my only driving force, and started holding myself accountable."

Kanishka Kumawat's father is a sports teacher, and from the time she was two or three he took her along on his walks and runs. Kicking a ball around beside him slowly became something more serious, and in time it became basketball.

She grew up in Rajasthan, where school rivalries were often built around sport. She played her first tournament at eight or nine in the Under 14 category, and that was when the game stopped being purely fun and became personal. Outside her father, support from the wider family was limited, and girls were not much encouraged at the grassroots level where she grew up. Because her closest relationships were with her parents, outside opinions rarely reached her the way they reached other girls.

Dropped at twelve

At twelve she received her first national call up, early for her age group and earned on merit. She was then dropped for a girl who had not even attended the trials. "That was my first real encounter with how unfair things could be," she says, "a genuine reality check." The next year she came back and scored around eighty percent in the trials, clearly ahead of the others. Her father could have used his position to speak up for her, and he chose not to. Nobody at the ground knew he was her father. She addressed him as "Sir", like everyone else.

The experience changed how she approached the game. "I told myself that being 'good enough' to be in the room wasn't the same as being irreplaceable," she says. She set out to become someone the team needed as much as she needed the team. After class 11 she was the most senior player left on a squad that had gradually thinned out, and she took on coaching on top of school and two practice sessions a day. She calls it a testing period.

Her father

Her father remained her steadiest influence, though not an effusive one. She says he treated her more strictly than he might have treated a son and rarely praised her outright. But she could see pride on his face after a good performance, and that was the only approval she looked for. As captain she asked her team to leave personal matters off the court. When people doubted their chances against stronger sides, she looked for her father in the stands, and catching his eye was enough to settle her.

One morning in particular stays with her. In the early days her father woke her for practice every day, and she often resisted. One day he called her name twice, got no answer and left without her. She woke on her own at about 8.30, an hour and a half late, and it shook her. For a moment it felt as if he had given up on her. She was ready in ten minutes and cycled to the ground alone, although she could barely ride a bicycle then. He never had to wake her again. "That was the moment I stopped relying on him as my only driving force," she says, "and started holding myself accountable."

Sport and study

From as early as class 6 she noticed an assumption at school that sportspeople were not serious students. She was once stopped from standing for class representative for that reason alone. She decided to prove it wrong and finished school with 98 percent, a result she pursued partly to make that point. Juniors have since told her they feel measured against the standard she set.

Basketball was never meant to be her profession. Her father, who built his own career in sport, was frank with her about the nepotism and politics he had seen in the system, and it was he who encouraged her not to make sport her career. Coming from a conservative joint Marwari family, she was drawn instead to work with wider reach, such as the civil services.

At LSR she kept close to full attendance even though practice started at 6 am, and she still calls her playing years there the best time of her life. The hardest stretch came afterwards, when she had to choose between playing at that intensity and focusing on her studies. Having lived by a strict routine since childhood, she finds unstructured time difficult. She now gives her studies the same hours she once gave to basketball, and admits it does not feel nearly as rewarding.

A pause

She finds it hard to separate herself from the game, having started playing at the age when identity begins to form. She sees her break as a pause rather than an end, and expects to come back to basketball one day, as a player or later as a coach. Asked to describe herself as an athlete, she names consistency, discipline, determination, and a refusal to accept failure until she has given her last effort. She treats every loss as a lesson, which is why she says she does not believe she has ever really lost.
