Kanishka Kumawat

The Discipline That Outlasted the Game

"I don't think I've ever really lost."

Kanishka Kumawat's earliest connection to basketball predates memory itself. Her father, a sports teacher, used to take her along on his walks and runs from the time she was two or three years old. What started as simply kicking a ball around slowly grew into something far more serious, carried forward by her father's introduction and her own willingness to stick with it.

Sport as Identity, Early and Often

Growing up in Rajasthan, she found herself in an environment where school rivalries were often built entirely around sport. She played her first tournament at eight or nine, in the Under-14 category, and that was when the sport first became personal rather than purely fun. Beyond her father, family support was limited; girls, she notes, weren't particularly encouraged at the grassroots level in the world she grew up in. But her closest relationships were with her parents, which meant outside opinions rarely reached her the way they reached other girls.

A Deliberate Choice Not to Go Professional

Despite basketball shaping so much of her life, pursuing it professionally was never really her goal. Her father, having built his own career in sport, was candid with her about the nepotism and politics he'd witnessed within the system, and it was he, ironically, who encouraged her not to make sport her profession. Coming from a joint, conservative Marwari family, she found herself drawn instead toward something with a wider impact, like the civil services. Sport stayed something personal and valued, rather than the larger goal she was working toward.

A First Real Reckoning With Unfairness

At twelve, she received her first national call-up, unusually early for her age group, having qualified fairly, on merit. She was then dropped in favour of a girl who hadn't even attended trials. "That was my first real encounter with how unfair things could be," she says, "a genuine reality check." The following year, she returned and scored around eighty percent in trials, clearly ahead of the competition. She could have used her father's position to advocate for her. He chose not to. Nobody at the ground even knew he was her father; she had to address him as "Sir," just like everyone else.

Becoming Indispensable

That early setback reshaped how she approached the sport. "I told myself that being 'good enough' to be in the room wasn't the same as being irreplaceable," she says. She decided she needed to become someone the team needed as much as she needed the team. That responsibility deepened after eleventh grade, when she became the senior-most player left on a team that had gradually thinned out. With coaching added to an already packed schedule of school and two daily practice sessions, it became, in her words, a genuinely testing period.

Her Father, Quietly Present

Throughout it all, her father remained her steadiest influence, though not a particularly effusive one. He treated her, she says, more toughly than he might have treated a son, rarely offering outright praise. But she could see pride on his face after a good performance, and that quiet recognition was the only validation she ever really sought. As captain, she asked her team to leave personal matters off the court and drew collective strength from unity rather than outside opinion. When outsiders doubted their chances against stronger teams, she simply looked toward her father in the stands, and that eye contact alone was enough to settle her.

A Quiet Turning Point

One memory stands out above all. In the early days, her father used to wake her for practice each morning, something she often resisted. One day, after calling her name twice without a response, he simply left without her. She woke on her own around 8:30, an hour and a half late, and the moment shook her; it felt, briefly, like he'd given up on her. She got ready within ten minutes and cycled to the ground alone, despite barely knowing how to ride a bicycle at the time. He never had to wake her again after that. "That was the moment I stopped relying on him as my only driving force," she says, "and started holding myself accountable."

Changing the Narrative, On Her Own Terms

As early as sixth grade, she noticed a lingering assumption at school that sportspeople weren't serious students. She was once kept from contesting a class representative election on that basis alone. Rather than let that perception stand, she resolved to change it herself, and left school with 98 percent, a result she pursued specifically to make that point. She's since heard from juniors who feel measured against the standard she set.

Life After the Court

The hardest mental stretch of her journey, she says, came after her playing years, when she had to choose between continuing at that intensity and focusing on academics. Having followed a tightly structured routine since childhood, she now finds unstructured time genuinely difficult to navigate. She's giving the same number of hours to studying that she once gave to sport, except now, she admits, it doesn't feel nearly as rewarding. During her playing years at LSR, despite practice that began at 6 am, she kept close to a hundred percent attendance, and still considers that period the best time of her life.

What Stays With Her

She struggles to draw a clear line between herself and the game, having begun playing at the very age when identity starts to form. She hasn't left the sport so much as paused it, and expects to return to it eventually, whether as a player again or, later, as a coach.

Asked to describe herself as an athlete, she settles on consistency and discipline, determination, and a refusal to accept failure until she's truly given her last effort. Every loss, by that logic, becomes a lesson rather than a defeat, which is perhaps why, in her own words, she doesn't believe she's ever really lost at all.
