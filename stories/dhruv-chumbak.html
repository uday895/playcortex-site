Dhruv Chumbak

Dhruv Chumbak and the Last Over He Asked to Bowl

"If you're mentally on top, you're fine."

Dhruv Chumbak was eleven when he played his first leather ball match and took six wickets in it. Until then cricket had been a game he played for fun with friends, no different from any other. After that match he dropped every other sport. "There's a difference between just playing a game, and actually thinking about it constantly," he says.

He is a left arm spinner from Delhi who also bats, and he grew up in a house full of athletes. His mother has played for India and was in contention for an Olympic team until a knee injury kept her on the sidelines. His father played cricket until an accident forced him to stop. His maternal uncle played for Railways, and whenever Dhruv felt low, his uncle was the person he called. "So basically I got three god-gifted people around me who understand me, properly understand me," he says.

Five wickets in one match

His first real test came at the DDCA Under 16 trials after Covid. All he knew was that five wickets in a match would take him to the next round. He took one in the first match and two in the second. He was fifteen, and he kept thinking that if he did not play for the state now, the other boys would go ahead without him.

Before the third match he changed how he thought about every delivery. "I'm bowling this, bro, this is my last ball, this is my last ball, I have to take this wicket," he told himself. He took six wickets and went through.

He finished that Under 16 season as Delhi's highest wicket taker, with 17 wickets in four or five matches, and was ranked around fourth in India. From there he went to the Zonal Cricket Academy high performance camp. He has since played three seasons of Under 19 cricket and is now part of the Delhi Premier League.

Pressure from above

Then he was left out of the sixth match, against Jammu and Kashmir. He had asked to play, because he expected three or four more wickets against a side that does not get much cricket, and he knew that could lift his ranking to first or second. One coach told him he was being rested. The boy picked in his place took four wickets, and when Dhruv raised it, he was told the coaches could not drop him now. "Bro, there's pressure from above, I can't do anything," one of them said. Dhruv went to his room and cried. His parents told him these were very small things, and having sportspeople at home meant it did not hit him as hard as it might have.

It has happened again since. At the end of one season he was given only the last match, against Tamil Nadu, and took four wickets. The following season he was not picked for the second or third games. In the fourth he took two wickets in the first innings and four in the second, and was dropped for the next match. Again he was told about pressure from above. He admits it drains his confidence. These days he asks the selectors directly what the problem is, and then tries to let it go within a minute. "Okay, politics is happening or not, fine, but I did my performance, I'm happy," he says. If he had been picked and failed, he adds, he could not have blamed politics.

Eight months away

He skipped his first Delhi Premier League season by choice, because he was playing red ball cricket for the Under 19 side and did not want to switch his focus to white ball cricket. In the second season his back gave way. Doctors told him not even to jog and advised surgery, which would have kept him out of cricket for around two years. He chose rehabilitation and strengthening instead, and told almost nobody about the injury.

During that period a coach decided to change his bowling action. Dhruv bowled the new way for five or six days. Then, bowling in the heat, he was in so much pain that he fell and could not get up. His muscles were short of water, they had been stretched too far and went into spasm, and the load went onto his lower back. His L4 and L5 vertebrae had shifted slightly out of place. He was told not to bowl for six months, and the whole episode lasted seven to eight months.

Staying at home was the hardest part. He would rather sit at the ground all day, and on Instagram he could see everyone else practising. He began to wonder whether he would be able to bowl at all. Each morning he told himself he would manage. The first ten days back felt wrong, as if his body was not working. After a month of continued rehab, he felt he was bowling better than he had before the injury.

The injury changed how he trains. He does back strengthening with resistance bands every day, and stretching and foam rolling after every session are now compulsory, "because I've had an injury before, so it's like a trauma for me now." He raises his workload by ten or twenty per cent at a time, never in big jumps.

Seven to defend

The match he remembers most was a night game with heavy dew. Seven runs were needed in the last over, and the batter was set on 120. Lalit Yadav, the IPL player, was about to take the ball. Dhruv told him the batter was right handed, Lalit was an off spinner, and he would bowl it himself. "You'll bowl? There's so much dew," Lalit said. The ball had to be wiped with a towel after every delivery.

With two needed off three balls, Dhruv kept a couple of fielders back and brought the rest in. The batter had been sweeping well, so he moved the fielder out of the sweep area on purpose and bowled outside off stump. The batter swept, got an inside edge and was bowled. The last ball was a dot. "I still remember, I took three-four minutes before bowling that ball," he says. "Because I feel our brain is very strong, but we don't use it."

How he prepares

The night before a match he is asleep by about 9:30 and up at five. He goes for a light jog of two or three kilometres, does some foam rolling, then meditates for ten minutes. He spends another ten minutes picturing the ground, the end he will bowl from, the batters he will face and the situations that might come up. On the drive he plays music loudly. "A cricketer, or any sportsperson, is only successful if they're chill," he says.

He likes to make practice harder than any match. In the West Delhi side, Nitish Rana sets targets such as scoring 15 runs in six overs. Dhruv tells him to try scoring ten off six balls instead. "As tough as I can make it for myself, I try to make it that tough," he says, so that whatever happens in a match feels easy.

After a match there is protein, electrolytes, stretching and an ice bath when one is available. On the drive home he thinks first about his mistakes, and then, as he gets close to home, about what went well. Even if a ball went for six, he reminds himself that he bowled the delivery he meant to bowl.

Off the field

Not everyone around him understands the life. A cousin tells him it must be easy to just go and play. Some people in his neighbourhood, a few of whom used to play cricket with him and gave it up, talk about him behind his back, and ask where he goes when he gets home at half past twelve after a night match. It gets into his head, and he does not like it.

His father works in the United States to support the family and Dhruv's cricket, while Dhruv lives in Delhi with his mother. He misses him a great deal. When he thinks about why his father is so far away, the thought that follows is simple: get there quickly.

Cricket still runs through his sleep. Until he was eighteen he would sometimes appeal in his dreams at two or three in the morning, and his mother once filmed it. He plays Under 19 cricket and is in the Delhi Premier League now, and he would like to see more 50 over leagues alongside the T20 ones, so young players learn the longer game. For himself, the measure is simple. "If you're mentally on top, you're fine."
