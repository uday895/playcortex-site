Dhruv Chumbak

The Spinner Who Plans Every Ball

"Because I feel our brain is very strong, but we don't use it."

Dhruv Chumbak started the way most Delhi kids do, playing cricket for fun with friends. At eleven he played his first leather-ball match and took six wickets. After that he dropped every other sport. "There's a difference between just playing a game and actually thinking about it constantly," he says.

Sport runs in the family. His mother played for India, his father played cricket until an accident ended it, and his maternal uncle played for Railways. Whenever he felt low, his uncle was the one he called.

Six Wickets When It Mattered

His first big test came at the DDCA Under-16 trials after Covid. He needed five wickets in a match to go through. He took one in the first game and two in the second. Before the third, he changed how he bowled. He told himself every delivery was his last chance. He took six wickets and made it through.

From there he went to the Zonal Cricket Academy high-performance camp. He finished the Under-16 season as Delhi's highest wicket-taker with 17 wickets in five matches, ranked around fourth among spinners in India. He has since played three years of Under-19 cricket and is now part of the DPL.

Dropped Without a Reason

Then he was left out of the next match against Jammu & Kashmir. The coaches told him there was "pressure from above." He was fifteen, and he went to his room and cried. His parents told him these were small things in a long career.

It has happened again since. He took four wickets against Tamil Nadu and wasn't picked for the next two games. When he finally got a game, he took six wickets and was dropped again. These days he asks the selectors directly what the problem is, then lets it go. "I did my performance, I'm happy. If he played me and I didn't perform, then I couldn't blame politics."

Eight Months Off the Field

A coach tried to change his bowling action. After five or six days of bowling the new way, in heat and short on water, his back spasmed and he collapsed on the pitch. His L4-L5 vertebrae had shifted. Doctors advised surgery, which would have meant two years without cricket.

He chose rehab instead, strengthening his back every day. The hardest part was watching everyone else train on Instagram while he sat at home. The first ten days back felt strange. A month later, he says, he was bowling better than he had before the injury.

Seven to Defend

In a dewy night match, his side had to defend seven runs in the final over against a batter on 120. He asked to bowl it. With two needed off three balls, he moved the fielder out of the sweep area on purpose and bowled wide of off stump. The batter swept and dragged it onto his stumps. "I took three-four minutes before bowling that ball, planning it in my head."

How He Prepares

The night before a match, he is asleep by 9:30. In the morning he meditates for ten minutes and then spends ten more picturing the ground, the batters and the situations he might face. On the drive back he thinks about his mistakes first, then about what went well, so he gets home in a good frame of mind.

His father works in the US so the family can support his cricket, and Dhruv misses him. It gives him one more reason to keep going. "If you're mentally on top, you're fine."
