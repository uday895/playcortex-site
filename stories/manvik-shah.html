Manvik Shah and the Ankle Injury Before Delhi University Trials

"Your best friend and your best enemy is within you itself."

One of Manvik Shah's earliest memories is watching the NBA on television and asking his mother what the sport was. He was tall for his age, so his parents enrolled him in an evening basketball programme at a school nearby. For a while it was simply something to keep him busy after school.

Within a year he liked the game enough to want more of it. A coach started basketball sessions at another nearby school, his father enrolled him, and he began training there at 5 in the morning on Saturdays and Sundays, alongside his school practice. He was in Class 5 or 6. In Class 7 he tried out for his school team and was selected.

The first nationals

His school played in the CISCE regionals and finished third or fourth. Afterwards Manvik learned he had been picked for the CISCE nationals, the first national tournament of his life. Playing against boys from other states showed him how high the standard was. He counts it as the real start of his basketball, and from then on people at school knew him for the game.

He wanted proper coaching, so he went to the basketball association and enrolled in formal training. Two weeks later Covid closed everything. He still played on a nearby court whenever the lockdown eased, and once it lifted he joined a club. "Now I could see good players, like really good players," he says. With better coaches around him his game improved quickly, and people started to notice.

Fitness and family

His first obstacle was off the court. He is vegetarian, and in his family the idea of going to a gym or taking whey protein was unfamiliar. His own knowledge was limited too. He ran a lot, but without the right diet the work did not show in his fitness, and it took him a long time to convince his parents.

He stopped playing during his Class 12 board exams. Once they were over, his parents agreed he could join a gym. He started training properly, tracking what he ate, and for the first time in a long while he could see his game getting better.

The injury

Then, last year, in the second quarter of an important tournament match, he tore a ligament in his ankle. It was his first serious injury, and it came the day before his 18th birthday. He had planned to celebrate. Instead he spent the day in hospital having an MRI scan. He remembers being angry and frustrated a great deal in that period, having lost his routine just as everything had started to work.

His Delhi University trials were a week away. He had only just learned to walk again and would have to run, with a real risk of injuring himself again. His parents backed his decision to go. There were arguments along the way, he says, but they stood by him, and the trials went well.

Starting again in Delhi

He had come from a state where basketball is not a big sport, and in Delhi his team included some of the best players in the city, with better fitness and more experience. Coming back from the injury, he was sidelined. He barely got time on court, even in practice matches. He accepts that this was fair, since he was not at his best, and he knew he would have to wait until his second year. He calls it a double edged sword: it was hard on him, but it also showed him how high the stakes were and how much work he needed to do.

By his own rough count, he has lost 60 to 70 percent of the matches he has played. The happiness of the wins is what pulls him through the rest. "You could be sad about it, but you shouldn't completely stop working hard," he says. After a mistake he tries to tell himself it is fine, then work out where he went wrong and learn from others.

He believes the hardest contest is with himself. "Your best friend and your best enemy is within you itself," he says. He thinks his personality was built by basketball: working hard, not giving up, treating an opponent as an opponent even if it is a friend, and accepting every teammate whether he likes them or not. Through effort, he says, he has reached multiple nationals, including CISCE and SGFI events, and a place in one of the strongest university teams.

Where he is now

He describes his current phase honestly as a bad one. He is away from home, without the comforts and resources he had there, still adjusting to a new place, and struggling to keep his fitness where he wants it. Days that begin at 5 or 6 in the morning with practice and then run through classes, society meetings, the gym, his diet and some kind of social life leave him drained. Even so, one good basket can outweigh the rest. If he stays away from the game for too long, he says, "I don't feel myself."

His short term goals are to get fitter, eat better and recover fully from the ankle injury, which is still not supporting him well. After that he wants to get back into his team's top five. He does not want to play for India; he sees a lot of politics in Indian basketball and wants to play as an amateur at college and university level. In the longer term he hopes to earn coaching certifications and become a good coach.

His advice to his younger self is to play his own game rather than judging himself by how others play, to work harder, and to get fitter sooner. Asked for words to describe himself as an athlete, he chose two: hardworking and calm.
