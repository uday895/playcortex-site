Arjun Singh Bhatia Is Chasing the Green Jacket

"That kid in a photo holding a polo stick, that's it, that's why I play golf."

Before Arjun Singh Bhatia ever held a golf club, he was swinging a small stick his family had made for him when he was three or four, modelled on the one his father used for horse polo. His grandfather, his Nanu, watched the boy's action and said, "Isko golf shuru karao," start him on golf. His father, an army officer, took the advice. In the army, golf was always within easy reach, and Arjun first held a club at five.

For the next few years he played perhaps once a month and did not think much of it. His first real memories of the game come from Jodhpur, when he was nine or ten, playing nine, fourteen or eighteen holes with his father and his father's colleagues once they were back from the office. His mother, a national level hockey player herself, saw it as a hobby. In 2015, after winning the monthly medal rounds at his home army club, he travelled for the first time to play an event, at ITC Grand Bharat in Delhi. He does not remember where he finished.

From fun to focus

Golf became more serious in 2017. He finished second at the North Zone event, qualified for the national circuit and had his first lesson with Jesse Grewal, who is still his coach. His first year on the Indian Golf Union's Main Tour was a struggle, but by the end of 2019 he had finished in the top three a couple of times. When Covid stopped play, he used the time to change his body and the way he practised. That was when he decided to take golf much more seriously, and when he first saw it as a possible career.

It meant growing up a little differently. In tenth and eleventh grade, when his friends started going out late, he was trying to keep up with schoolwork, because he was so rarely in school. His parents insisted academics came first: if his grades fell, his practice time would be cut, though it never came to that. At St. Kabir Public School in Chandigarh, his administrator, Mr Bakshi, helped him fit assignments and deadlines around tournaments. "There was a sense of being different, not inferior, not superior, just different," he says. He would not change it.

His first big win came in 2021, when he won the national qualifier for the amateur circuit at his second home club, beating Shaurya Bhattacharya, a future India number one amateur, head to head in the final round. He had been finishing near the top for years, but this was his first win on a big scale. In 2023 he became India's number one in the Under 18 category. The Golf Foundation had supported him since 2019, and sponsorships followed from Oil India and Titleist. He noticed that people he did not know became more accommodating as the results came. His friends did not change at all. "We still talk trash to each other, still joke around," he says.

There was a physical cost too. Around the 2021 and 2022 seasons, after losing a lot of weight during Covid, he grew a couple of inches and added muscle while making swing changes, and his wrists suffered. He worked through it with the physiotherapist Navnita Gautam, who also works with RCB, and with his coach on adjustments to his swing. At the time he mostly played through the pain. "I was too young to really process an injury," he says. He has had no major injuries in the last three or four years.

A big fish in open water

He arrived at Long Island University ranked number one in India by about 80 points, when each event was worth around 40. His coach called him a big fish in a small pond. His freshman year was his worst in five years. The greens and conditions in the US were very different from home, and his scoring average rose to around 73.4, about two strokes worse than six months earlier. He was disappointed but never thought about quitting. "I never treated it as something I had to do. It's something I enjoy."

The bigger adjustment was off the course. In India he had his parents, his coach, caddies and help at home, and he had never had to make his bed or do his laundry. In college, everything was on him. He says it has made him a more mature person off the course than on it. His college coach at Long Island University, Ben, helped him learn to manage his time and his emotions, sometimes by being tough on him. "Tough love is sometimes all you need," Arjun says.

He went two and a half years in college without a win. Each time he came close and fell short, he repeated the same message. "I'd tell myself I'm just going to keep showing up, keep giving myself these chances, because if you keep knocking on that door, someone's going to open it eventually." On hard days he spent hours on the phone with his father, who told him it was fine and that he would figure it out. The win finally came in April, after a lot of work with his psychologist, and it mattered for his belief in himself as a college player.

He has come to respect how much of golf happens in the head. In tennis, the next point arrives before there is time to dwell on a mistake. In golf there is plenty of time to dwell. After a bad round he talks it through with his coach, then asks himself whether the problem was mental, physical, a lack of practice or a lack of commitment to a shot. He also admits that in his freshman year he did not practise well and wasted time on his phone, and he wishes he had found that discipline sooner. "A night out on a Friday or Saturday won't change your life, but do that over a year and it will."

The people behind him

His father has been at every lesson and every tournament and, Arjun says, knows his game inside out, probably better than he does. When his father was posted away and Arjun had to stay in Chandigarh, his mother drove him to practice every day and stayed with him through his lessons. He admits he annoyed her and fought with her a lot, and says her sacrifices helped him more than anything. Asked what has never changed in all his years in the game, he names his parents' support.

The life has its costs. Over the last five years he has missed countless birthdays, anniversaries and family dinners, and there are friends he calls only every few months. Off the course he keeps a small, close circle, loves music and old Bollywood films, and wants to beat everyone at everything, whether it is cricket, football or basketball.

What comes next

Arjun is now transferring to the University of North Texas for his senior year, a historically top 50 programme and former national champions that plays the biggest schools in the country. Beyond that, his goals are to turn professional, play on the PGA Tour and win the Masters. He says he practises every day with Augusta in mind rather than the next week's tournament. "That kid in a photo holding a polo stick, that's it, that's why I play golf."

Asked to describe himself in five words, he chose persistent, committed, arrogant, competitive and passionate, and explained the third: "If you don't believe you can beat everyone, there's no chance you will."
