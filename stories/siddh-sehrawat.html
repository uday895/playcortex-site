Siddh Sehrawat's Seven Years in Archery

"It was like this is the best thing that has ever happened to me."

Siddh Sehrawat was in Class 6 in 2015 when he and his younger brother started archery together in Chandigarh. Their father wanted both boys to take up one sport seriously, and they had already tried five or six, including basketball, badminton and swimming. Then their maternal uncle, whose own children played chess, came to visit from Delhi. At the Lake Club he came across archery and thought an individual sport would suit both boys. Their mother signed them up.

The start was slow. For the first six months they were not allowed to shoot at all. They stood in one place for hours pulling a rubber band to build strength and form. "It's extremely boring," he says. The brothers quit, came back, and quit again. It took six or seven months before Siddh was given his first bow, and it was only around Class 9 that he decided to take the sport seriously. From then on the two of them trained for three to four hours every evening and did not miss a day. They ran and did push ups together, and having each other there, he says, helped the hours pass.

A new coach and a new level

His first real result came soon after he committed: two golds and a silver at the CBSE North Zone championship. His mother decided it was time to aim higher and moved both boys from the government coaching centre to a private coach.

That meant starting again. The new coach had him pull a rubber band for six months, then pull his first professional bow for another six without shooting, and then spend six more months shooting at distance before he was allowed to compete. Siddh found it boring enough to wonder about going back to his old centre, but he could see the coach was building his base. The difference in attention was large. The government coach would give instructions and leave. The private coach watched every arrow Siddh shot and kept an eye on his life away from the range as well.

The results followed. Out of a possible 360, Siddh had been shooting around 230 to 280 and finishing somewhere between 80th and 90th at national level. With the new coach his scores rose to 330, 340 and 345, and his national ranking climbed to around 10th or 12th. That mattered, because only the top 12 or 24 archers go on to the next stage, where scholarships and other support become available. He won medals at the state championship every year, including gold in two age categories and bronze in the Under 19 event at Moga in Punjab.

Then Covid stopped everything. He could not practise for close to two years, and when he returned his skills had slipped. He had been expecting a medal in the Under 14 category, but by the time competitions resumed he had aged out of it without ever competing in it properly. He moved into the sub junior category, where archers shoot at 60 metres, and it took him about two years to be ready for national competition there.

The Asia Cup trials

By Class 10 he was shooting well again, but his parents wanted him to concentrate on his board exams while his coach was telling him to prepare for the Asia Cup trials. He gave the year to his studies. When the exams were over he had three months left, and his coach was frank with him: other archers had been training continuously, and even his brother was three or four steps ahead and had a better chance of being selected.

For those three months Siddh more or less lived at the ground. He slept at a friend's home nearby and his mother brought him food. By the end of it, his coach was telling him he was the one with a real chance.

At the trials in Sonipat he made the cut for the top 32, then the top 24. The others who had travelled from his ground were knocked out along the way. The next day he made the top 16. On the last day there were two more rounds of scoring to decide the top nine, who would receive scholarships and a place in the Khelo India scheme, with the top four going on to the Asia Cup. Selection would also have meant training at a national institute with equipment, food and accommodation provided.

He finished his final round and went home sure that he would be fifth or sixth. Looking back, he calls those days the moment he felt most alive in the sport. "In the last I was the only one who was there left in the top 16 candidates, I felt really alive, bro."

A five instead of a six

When the list came out, his name was in 16th place. His coach rang to ask what had happened. Siddh did not know, and they rushed back to the stadium to check. At those trials the archers recorded their own scores. His total should have been around 675 out of 720, but he had written a 5 where there should have been a 6, and his total had come out a hundred points lower. Instead of finishing fifth or sixth, he was 16th and out of the top nine.

His coach and seniors were disappointed, and he was scolded for throwing the chance away. It had been his final year in the sub junior category, at what he considers the peak of his archery. He describes what came next as a downfall.

He kept training seriously through Classes 11 and 12. His parents agreed that archery would come first and that his aim at school would simply be to pass. He trained three sessions a day, morning, afternoon and late at night, fitted a tuition class into the gap in between, and went to school once or twice a week. His marks fell from 90 percent in Class 10 to 75 percent the following year, a cost he had accepted in advance.

The move up to the junior category brought another change of distance, from 60 to 70 metres, and he found it hard to adjust. He came close at the National School Games in Class 12, finishing fourth, but only the top three received medals. Over the same period his brother won medals in the sub junior, junior and senior categories. Siddh says this neither discouraged him nor made him desperate to catch up. When he had been free to train, he had been the better archer, and when his brother had his Class 10 exams, the roles were reversed.

Archery never caused him a serious injury, though drawing the bow brought regular pain in his triceps and shoulders that needed massage. His coach told him his mental game was stronger than anyone else's. What he had to keep working on was the physical side: standing for three hours in the sun and shooting 72 arrows consistently.

From the bow to the pistol

After seven years, he decided it was time to move on. "For archery, I have given seven years, it had not given me that kind of a result I was hoping for," he says. He has not sold his equipment, which he and his brother still have, and he says he can start again if he ever feels archery was the better sport for him. For now he has switched to pistol shooting. Two months in, his coach says the aim he developed in archery gives him an advantage, and while others who joined with him are still learning to aim, he has already been given a personal pistol.

Siddh is 18 and studying for a B.Com LLB at UILS. He wants to become a judge, and he is also interested in joining the police as an IPS officer. His father has advised him to keep competing, in archery or in shooting, because a sports record can help through the sports quota on both paths. Asked to describe himself as an athlete, he chose dedicated, consistent, serious and hard working. Once he commits to something, he says, he wants to finish it properly.
