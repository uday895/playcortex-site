Krish Girdhar Plays Bigger Than Six Feet

"You cannot wake up one day and decide to leave the sport."

Krish Girdhar remembers the date of his first basketball practice: 6 December 2016. Before that he had done gymnastics, where he could manage flips and backflips but could not touch his toes. Several of his school friends lived in the same apartment complex and played basketball every evening. They were far better than him, but he was tall and athletic, and at his small school he already played almost every sport on offer. His parents encouraged him to try anything, which he says helped him get comfortable with the idea that it was all right to be bad at something.

What drew him in was watching the school team: the tournaments they travelled to, the respect they got from teachers and how much fun they had together. He had never played a team sport. His best friend was on the boys' team and told him to give it a shot, so he went along to a morning session. "Since then it's just been basketball," he says.

Earning a place

His school coach was strict about who stayed on the team. The players used to say it was harder to stay on than to get on, because anyone who stopped showing up or stopped performing lost their spot. Six months after Krish started, the coach asked for his jersey size. As with everyone else, his name would not go on the shirt until he had won something. By then he had begun beating some of the regular players at practice, and the coach had started giving him personal attention. Seeing other people put their time into him, he says, made him think, "I must be worth something."

The team went through 2017 without losing a tournament. Their only defeat that year came in a group stage match. With the school he travelled to tournaments across India, including Woodstock in Mussoorie and Ramjas in Delhi, and he was selected twice for camps in Serbia, once for guards and once for general skills.

In 2018 the school played a friendly against the one side that had beaten them. The coach benched the senior players and told the newer ones to go out and show what they could do, with no pressure. Krish scored somewhere between 25 and 28 points. He hit several threes, blocked shots and tried moves he had only ever practised on his own. The game became exciting enough that the cricket coaches came to watch as well, and coaches at the school still bring it up when he visits. "It still feels so surreal to me when I talk about it, because I had no clue I could do all of that."

The lesson he took from it was a simple one. "You can practice as much as you want," he says. "But when you get the ball and if you're not confident with the ball in your hand, you will not go anywhere."

Nearly walking away

In eleventh grade, Krish and two teammates decided they wanted to quit. They trained in the morning, again after school and sometimes in between, seven days a week, and at sixteen it left them no room for anything else. Krish says he was set on stopping. His parents reminded him how long he had played and how much he loved it, and suggested he cut back to three days a week, once a day, rather than leave altogether. He took up MUN and other sports, and by the end of that year all three of them were back on court. Covid then stopped play for about a year, and still none of them quit. "You cannot wake up one day and decide to leave the sport," he says.

He has never put much pressure on himself before games. He does not set targets such as scoring 50 points or playing for India, because he thinks that would only push him into bad shots. "Only reason I play basketball is so I can have fun, and the day I stop having fun is the day I will stop, probably, playing." He believes that freedom is part of why he played well and why people from higher levels began to notice him.

Taking the hits

At six feet, Krish is undersized on a basketball court, and he plays accordingly: physical, taking contact, chasing every rebound. In one tournament he took it on himself to guard a six foot eight opponent who had been dominating every game. The player managed about two points against him and fouled out, frustrated at how often he had fouled Krish. "I like to take the hits. I like to get physical. I like to get dirty, dive for every loose ball on the ground." Whatever the score, he plays until the final whistle.

What kept him going through years of daily training was the people beside him. If his teammates were giving everything, he felt he had to give the same, for them, for his coaches and for the family and friends who came to watch. "There's no way that I can let them down," he says.

Injury and overtraining

In his third year of college he suffered a disc bulge, which led to sciatica that still troubles him. For around a year and a half he was in and out of hospitals, imaging centres and doctors' clinics. Some days he could barely get out of bed, and he could not go out with his friends. He put on a lot of weight, and for someone who always wants to be moving, it was a hard time. He has since got back into good physical shape.

During that period he kept a diary, tracking his pain each day and the methods that eased it. The tracker became a startup idea, which he pitched to an incubator. It was funded, and he won a government grant under Startup India from the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology. He is no longer working on it, but he says the experience took him places he never thought he would go.

He now believes overtraining played a part in the injury. In college there was a hard session every morning and another every evening, and on a big campus he walked 20,000 to 30,000 steps a day. He could eat anything and still not gain weight or strength. Since recovering he has learned to read the signs his body gives him, and on the wrong day he will swap an intense game for a swim.

College also changed how he played. Coming back after the long Covid break, he spent his first year and a half doing his duty as a team player and passing the ball around well. Once he found his feel again, he started attacking the ring. He made plenty of mistakes, and his teammates and coaches were sometimes frustrated, but he sees those mistakes as the building blocks that made him a much better player by the end of college. It is the advice he would give his younger self: be aggressive from the first day. "Better make mistakes when you're a young guy than when you're 22 about to graduate."

Life after college

Krish graduated in May 2025 and now works a desk job. He goes to the gym at least four times a week and fills the gaps with pickleball, swimming and runs, and he recently trekked to Annapurna Base Camp with friends who have played sport all their lives. Basketball is harder to fit in, because it needs a full team. He used to play four to six hours a day, and what he misses most is the fixed evening session: "it's 7 p.m. and a bunch of us, 15, 20 of us, are going to show up on the court and we're going to have the time of our life." He says he is slowly getting used to life without it.
