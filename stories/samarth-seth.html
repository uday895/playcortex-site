Samarth Seth

Samarth Seth, the fast bowler who became a Delhi opener

"It's about two or three good innings and things can change for you instantly."

Samarth Seth started out as a fast bowler. He represented Delhi in the Under 14s in 2012, then a back injury forced him to give up bowling altogether, and he began again as an opening batter. Two years later he was batting for Delhi Under 16. Known to most people as Sam, he has played senior domestic cricket since 2018, scored hundreds at home and abroad, and when we spoke he was ten days away from another Delhi Premier League season with Purani Delhi, who had retained him after he finished as their highest run scorer.

A tennis ball and his father

Like most Indian cricketers, he started with a tennis ball, playing with his father. At around nine, in about 2010, his father took him to an academy, and from then on took him everywhere: to matches, to academies, and to practice sessions before school. "My family has always been very supportive," he says. Asked whether cricket was his father's dream or his own, he says it began with his father seeing something in him. "Later on I also felt the same. So I think it's our dream."

After the back injury, he worked only on his batting from 2012. In 2014 he played for Delhi Under 16 as an opener. In a match against Jammu, with Delhi chasing more than 500, he scored around 170 and helped win the game. "From then on I thought that I can do this professionally," he says.

The commitment meant missing things. Practice started early in the morning, and there were many places he simply did not go. He says friends and classmates were mostly neutral about it, and he never paid much attention to what they thought. He had been clear from the start about what he wanted.

Arunachal, England and Sri Lanka

He played Under 19 cricket for Delhi twice, then moved to Arunachal Pradesh as a guest player, representing them in List A, first class and T20 cricket. He made his debut in 2018 and scored a hundred in that first match, then followed it with a strong white ball season and a good second year.

In 2021 he came back to Delhi. It is a big setup with many established players, and chances were scarce. He played Under 23 and Under 25 cricket and made 80 against Haryana in the CK Nayudu Trophy, but the opportunities did not keep coming. He began spending seasons abroad as an overseas professional, first in the UK, then in Sri Lanka, where he scored three first class hundreds in a row, and in 2024 in Northern Ireland, where his club won everything that season. Representing Delhi as a boy was his first big achievement, he says. Those three hundreds in Sri Lanka were the next.

Back home, he played the first season of the Delhi Premier League for East Delhi Riders, who won the title, and has spent the last two seasons with Purani Delhi.

The years away

Asked about a setback, he points to a decision rather than a bad match. At 21 he started going to the UK on six month professional deals, and he now thinks he should have waited. Being away for half the year meant missing Delhi's domestic circuit and all its tournaments, at a time when staying in view of the selectors mattered. "When you go out you certainly get a little out of the circuit," he says. Since he stopped playing abroad and committed fully to Delhi, he says his mindset has completely changed. "It's about two or three good innings and things can change for you instantly."

He is honest that cricket brings more bad days than good ones, and that on some of those days he has wondered whether the game is right for him. "Eventually you get to know that you cannot live without that because you've done it for so many years," he says. He has had fractures, a dislocated finger and an operation, and once broke a finger before a first class game. "Injuries are part of the process. It will happen to everyone."

The temple and the training

When things go badly he does not need many words. He goes to the temple, every second day in fact, and sits there until his energy returns. "It keeps me sane," he says. He thinks Indian cricket leaves players little room to admit they are struggling, because the competition is so intense and there is always someone ready to replace them. "It's very normal to have a mental breakdown, reassess things, and I think it happens with everyone, but no one really talks about it."

For more than two years he has worked with a strength and conditioning coach and lost 10 to 12 kilograms. The competition has risen sharply in recent years, he says, and he has had to raise his own standards to keep up with 19 year olds. He turned vegetarian three years ago and now eats almost exactly the same food every day, planned by his trainer: oats with protein and fruit in the morning; brown rice, dal, curd and the soya and paneer tikki his mother makes for lunch; a protein shake in the evening; and quinoa, dal and vegetables at night. As a boy he would eat anything, including plenty from outside. If he had had his current mindset and his current body at 16, he believes, he would already have achieved what he is still chasing.

His cricket coach has been the one constant. They have worked together since Sam was 13, and it was this coach who turned him from a bowler into a batter. "Everything I've achieved and whatever I've done is because of his guidance," he says.

Off the field

Away from cricket he describes himself as calm and composed, happiest within a small circle. He likes music and travel, and his Instagram feed is mostly suits, watches, shoes and cars. He admits he will buy a suit with no wedding in sight. Six months ago, given unlimited money, he would have bought a car. Now he would put it into land, SIPs and gold. If he were not playing, he thinks he would be working in business, perhaps helping with the family business. He wants to travel while he is young: Europe, the Northern Lights and a skydive are all on the list.

His immediate goal was to be the best player in the DPL and push for an IPL contract. The longer ambition has not changed since he was a boy. "Playing for India is the biggest dream," he says, and after that, staying there at the highest level.
