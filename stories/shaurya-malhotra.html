Shaurya Malhotra and the Habit He Cannot Break

"You've made your life badminton, you can't live without it."

Shaurya Malhotra tried golf, tennis and squash before badminton, each one introduced by his father. Around the age of ten, badminton clicked. "It's a high-intensity sport, it requires speed and reflexes, so it was my type of game." He started through an early morning coaching programme at school, getting up at 6 to play. Even before proper coaching began, his father would wake him at 5 for a run and then casual games with his uncle and others. Those early starts became a habit he has never lost. His mother got up with him too, cooking his meals before and after practice and arranging whatever he needed before tournaments.

He was very unsure of himself at first. At his first tournament, Yojan, he lost in the first round after a full year of practice, and it shook him. "From the start, I just didn't want to lose, and that kept me going." Two years in, at an Under 15 district tournament, he reached the doubles final and the singles semi final. That was when he decided to stay with the sport.

Six to eight hours a day

For years he trained six to eight hours a day and was barely at school. His parents wanted him to keep up both studies and sport without missing school, and it took time to convince them otherwise. His days were long: up at 4:15, training at 5, the school bus at 8, home by 4, training again at 5, home at 9, then homework, dinner and sleep by 10:30. He spent a month at Thailand's national camp, with his parents beside him, and about six months at Prakash Padukone's academy in Bangalore, training with national level and ranked players. He says those two stints built the base of his game.

He won district titles in singles and doubles at Under 17 and Under 19 level, won his zone, took a state bronze in singles and reached a highest ranking of fourth seed in Under 19 across Uttar Pradesh. None of it satisfied him for long. "It was never enough for me, that was my mentality," he says. "It was an uncontrollable hunger I used to carry."

So close, twice

He never earned direct entry to the nationals, so he played All India qualifying events, where players fight through several rounds to reach the main draw. In his last year in the Under 19 category there were two tournaments back to back, in Jaipur and Bangalore. They were his last chance to build an All India ranking as a junior, which could have opened the door to international competition. He lost in the final qualifying round in Jaipur, and then the same thing happened in Bangalore.

He felt lost and did not touch his racket for a month or two. The break only made it worse. He had played the same game for eight years, and stopping left his mind going over how close he had come and what he could have done differently. Eventually he told himself to go again, and he has not stopped since.

Into men's badminton

Once the Under 19 years were over, the pressure became real. He had already played some men's events, but now every tournament was fully open, and anyone could beat him. He remembers an Under 23 match just after he turned 19, against an opponent over six feet tall, the first time he had faced that kind of power. Injuries came with the step up, to his back, his shoulder and his right ankle.

Last year, in the third qualifying round of an All India tournament in Bangalore, he twisted his ankle in the first set. It swelled up and turned black. He played the set out anyway and lost, then spent two weeks in bed. With the size of the men's field adding to the pressure, he thought seriously about quitting. Then one day, once he had recovered, he simply went back. "It's a habit, a loop you get into, you've made your life badminton, you can't live without it."

College brought a different kind of change. He is studying for a BMS degree at Shiv Nadar University, and living in a hostel, without his coaching set up and without his parents, he was on his own for the first time. He kept his early routine, and now trains about five hours a day instead of eight, "but those five hours are quality hours." He says the real lesson was never about balancing studies and sport but about controlling his time. He used to write out timetables hour by hour, and he still keeps a diary of what he did each hour and what his goals are.

Learning calm

As a younger player he was very hard on himself. He overthought mistakes and cried after losses. Playing men's matches during his Under 19 years taught him that staying calm on the big points brings him closer to a kind of flow. "At crucial points, the calmer you are, the more clearly you can think," he says. After a mistake he notes what he could have done differently and turns to the next point. "What's done is done, it can't be changed." He tries to give his best whether or not he is in a good mood, and however sore he feels.

His favourite player is Lee Chong Wei, who never won Olympic or World Championship gold and was so often the runner up. Shaurya says he connected with that emotionally, and he used to imitate Lee's strokes in practice. He feels most alive when a crowd gathers for an intense match, and on days when his smashes land exactly where he wants them. Off court he is outgoing and happy to spend a day off with friends. On court, he says, he is a different person, extremely focused.

His advice to his younger self is simple: "don't be so hard on yourself." He still gets up at 6 to run, play, go to the gym and play again. After twelve years in the sport, his aim is to qualify at All India level, build a proper ranking and move towards international competition. Outside badminton, he wants to start his own business one day.
