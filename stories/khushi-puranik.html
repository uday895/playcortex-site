Khushi Puranik

Khushi Puranik and the Sound of the Ball

"My name is Khushi, meaning happiness, and I used to be genuinely happy inside the squash court."

Khushi Puranik's father is in the Air Force, and her family had one rule: every new posting meant trying something new. She earned a brown belt in karate, went skating and played tennis for four years in Chandigarh. When her father was posted to Delhi, she kept playing tennis on the side while watching him play squash and hitting the ball with him. He thought there was something in the way she struck it, and he became her first coach. She was eleven.

They entered her in an Under 13 tournament with no real idea of the standard, and walked in assuming she would beat everyone. She remembers watching her opponent warm up and being unable to swallow. "All I remember is stepping onto the court and blacking out completely," she says. It happened in her first three tournaments. She would walk on court, forget everything she knew about squash and freeze.

Her father suggested meditation, to centre her breathing and bring her focus back to one place. After that she stopped blanking out, and the confidence carried off court too. She had been very shy and closed off. Now she began making friends easily, and people started calling her Khushi "Cool" Puranik.

A stranger's belief

In time her father stepped back from coaching her. As she puts it, a person can really only be a dad or a coach at one time. Then, at the DDA Sports Complex in Delhi, a man who had been watching her play offered to coach her out of his own goodwill. He was not a professional coach and worked in the corporate sector, but he said he saw potential in her. "A complete stranger putting belief in me like that, I think that was my wake-up moment."

She calls her father the biggest inspiration of her life. His own childhood was hard, and he had wanted to play sport when he was young, but his circumstances were nothing like hers. Hearing his story, she says, was her biggest motivation.

Her first big result came with Delhi's team at the SGFI Games, run by the School Games Federation of India, where they finished second. In the Under 15 category she took a game off the player then ranked number three in India. Over the following years she reached number 8 in India in Girls Under 19, number 12 in the women's category and 243 in the world. After school she won a full sports scholarship to Shiv Nadar University, where she studied International Relations.

College and the court

The scholarship meant training twice, and sometimes three times, a day. Her classmates spent their days in the library; she never had that time, and for a long while she avoided people from her course because she felt "dumb" in front of them. That feeling faded slowly. Her classmates, it turned out, thought what she was doing was impressive and admired how hard she worked. Once an international event was held on her own campus, and she played an Egyptian opponent while her professors and friends watched. "It was a nice feeling, even though I lost, to put on a show in front of them."

In 2023 she played for Delhi at the 37th National Games in Goa. Her parents were in Pune, so that Diwali she stayed at a friend's place in Delhi instead of going home. It was the same day she learned she had broken into the top eight in the country, which in the junior category meant a call to the Indian team trials. At a party that night, dancing in Crocs, she twisted her leg twice and tore a ligament. She spent around two months in bed. "I felt very stupid," she says, partly because of how ironic the injury was. For a while she wondered whether it was a sign that she should let the sport go. The injury permanently weakened her dominant leg, and it flared up again later when she twisted it on court.

Losing the joy, and a title

Over her college years, squash slowly turned into an obligation. The scholarship meant she could not step away even when she wanted to, and she describes it as "a vicious trap". She also found the national circuit increasingly unpleasant to be part of. "My name is Khushi, meaning happiness, and I used to be genuinely happy inside the squash court," she says. Towards the end she felt trapped inside the four walls. "I need to get out of here, I can't breathe."

It was a very difficult period, and her sense of her own worth had become tied to how she played. In the middle of it, with her leg troubling her again, she played what she calls probably her best tournament and won her first national title. The final went to five games, and she won the decider. "I never thought I'd win coming from that mental state," she says. "Walking out afterward, I felt like I'd crossed such a big hurdle in my life."

A fighter on court

Her inner voice changed over the years. Early on she just wanted to finish quickly and get off court. Later it became: breathe, take it in, you've got this. In tight moments she tries to make herself laugh. "I'll tell myself, 'What are you doing, silly,' and then say, okay, no worries, next point is yours." She admits that after a loss she might blame the referee or the court in public, but deep down she knows it is usually a lack of training.

Her last tournament before stepping away was in Bangalore. She was two games down, fought back to two games all and lost the fifth 11 to 9. She says she often feels most alive after a loss, as long as she knows she gave everything, and that match was one of those times. "I felt so alive, like a true fighter."

After competitive squash

Khushi has graduated and has not played since. She does want to play again, just not professionally, and says she loves the sport now that there is no performance pressure attached. She lives at the Air Force Station in Nagpur, which has a squash court, and hopes to start coaching young children there. Squash is very expensive, and she knows how much her parents spent, so she would charge little, perhaps two thousand rupees a child. She is also preparing for a master's and plans to move towards sports management.

She credits her whole family. Her mother drove her to training after school and sat watching; she was, Khushi says, "my driver, my dietician, everything". Her father registered her for tournaments and managed her prize money. Her younger sister gave up her drama club and other things whenever the four of them travelled for a tournament.

Away from squash she crochets, paints and plays with her cat. She admits she still does not fully know who she is without the sport, because for so long people saw her with a racket first. Through the hardest years, though, she kept getting up to play, and she knows why. "The love I had for the sport, for the sound of the ball, for the movement on court. That stayed." She hopes to find her way back to sport by a different route. "Once an athlete, always an athlete, and even though the conventional path in sport didn't work out for me, if it works out in a non-conventional way, I'd be the happiest."
