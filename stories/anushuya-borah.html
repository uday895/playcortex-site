Anushuya Borah

Anushuya Borah's sixteen years in the water

"I think the one thing that's never changed about me is that I never stopped choosing swimming."

Anushuya Borah was four when she started swimming, and it was not her idea. She had suffered from severe asthma since birth, and when her parents took her to doctors in Chennai, they were told swimming was the only way it could be healed. It helped that she already loved water. As a small child she was drawn to beaches and spent hours in the bathtub at home. "Asthma was actually the main reason I took up swimming," she says.

Sixteen years later she is a national medallist from Assam, a student in Delhi, and still in the pool.

Six years without a medal

She was part of the first batch at a new pool, and some of her school friends soon followed. For a while it was simply a relaxing hour after school. Three months in, she crossed the fifty metre mark and was moved up to the advanced group. Her coach, who had also coached her mother, told the family she now needed to come regularly rather than three days a week. Her parents agreed, though they had no plans yet for her to become a professional.

She began competing and kept improving, but frequent health problems held her back. Between 2010, when she started, and 2016 she did not win a single medal. "I lost hope and felt I wasn't meant for this," she says. Her parents kept telling her the work would pay off one day, and she kept going.

In 2016 her parents moved her to a new coach from outside their district. The arrangement was kept secret, and she trained at resorts and in ponds out among farmland, away from onlookers. That year she was selected for her first nationals, the school nationals in Delhi, and finished in the top eight of her event. It was the result that persuaded her to take swimming seriously.

The breakthrough came in 2018 at a club competition in Sonipat, Haryana, where she won her first national gold medal. "That was the turning point, I remember thinking, now I cannot quit," she says. More medals followed at nationals and state meets, along with group championships, and in 2020 she finished in the top ten at the Khelo India Youth Games in Guwahati.

Her mother's routine

The family lived in Jorhat, a small town, and the pool was almost 29 kilometres from home. Her school was close to the pool, but the travel was exhausting, so her father eventually bought an apartment nearby. Every morning a driver took her to school while her mother made the journey separately by public transport. Her mother brought her lunch, fed her, took her to practice and then to tuition, and the two of them usually got home around 9 or 9:30 at night. "She did this every single day," Anushuya says. "None of what I've achieved would have been possible without her."

Her mother also ran her diet, without a nutritionist. Anushuya had been underweight for her sport, and swimmers have to keep their weight within a narrow range. Meals at home were simple, with rice as the staple and protein from chicken, duck, mutton, pigeon and pulses such as moong and masoor dal. "Being from the Northeast, food has never been too complicated for me," she says.

The cost was a childhood with little room for anything else. From sixth grade onwards she barely went to school, and she cannot remember sitting an exam other than her finals. She missed birthday parties and never went on holidays. "I think the biggest difference between me and other children my age back then was that I had no playtime," she says.

Covid, and then Delhi

When Covid closed the pools in 2020, she lost the one thing a swimmer cannot do without. "Even a few days out of the pool and you start feeling heavy in the water," she says. Her father built a small swimming area at home so she could keep training with the coach she had worked with at the resorts and ponds.

The harder period came after she moved to Delhi for college. She no longer had her coach, her mother was not there to manage her diet, and food, travel and coaching were all more difficult. The academic work, she says, was never the problem. She was selected for the All India University Games in her first year but was not satisfied with how she swam. In the shape she used to be in, she believes, she would have won a medal.

Her results dropped, and she found it hard to cope without her family nearby. "Normally my mother is the one who cheers me up, but I didn't have anyone there," she says. "That period in Delhi was actually tougher for me than the Covid period itself, because I had to go through it alone." Her mother would not let her quit, reminding her how long she had been doing this.

One moment stayed with her. In March she had to miss the Khelo India Women's competition because her college had reopened. Afterwards she saw that her swim mates had competed, checked the medal timings and realised she could have won a national medal if she had been there. She calls it a turning point. This year, training at home again, she feels after a recent competition that she is finally getting back to where she used to be.

Pressure, noise and the blocks

Unlike many athletes, she does not see pressure as a problem. "If there's no pressure, I can't perform well," she says. "I need a certain level of pressure, otherwise I don't perform the way I want to." She has noticed the same thing away from the pool: when she is too relaxed, even ordinary work turns out worse.

Noise is another matter. Competitions are loud, and at summer meets the heat adds to the yelling and cheering. She finds it overstimulating, so before a race she keeps to herself, usually sitting in a corner with her mother, listening to music or talking quietly with a few close friends. She avoids discussing the competition itself. Then, standing behind the blocks, she looks at the water and her lane and pictures herself swimming the way she wants to, hitting the timings she is aiming for. "I picture the whole race in my head, and then when I'm actually racing, that's what I draw on," she says.

She now has access to strength and conditioning and sports psychology at Abhinav Bindra Targeting Performance, which is free for national level participants. Before a meet she has deep tissue massage, cupping or dry needling to keep her muscles from knotting. Afterwards there is slow recovery swimming and ice.

Still choosing swimming

Outside the pool she danced for ten years and holds a degree in contemporary and ballet dance. She plays the drums and loves painting. Growing up in a small town, she was often told there was no future in sport apart from cricket. Recently one of her old teachers told her she used to be very unserious about her studies. Anushuya was taken aback, because everything she has today came through swimming. Now that people have seen what she has achieved, the sport has become a source of pride for her family.

Swimming has not always come first. She received an offer from the University of Sydney to study sports management, but she is an only child and did not want to leave her mother alone. Otherwise, she says, she would probably be training in Australia by now.

There was a period when swimming became more about competition than enjoyment, and the fun faded for a while. It came back, and she believes that is why she never quit, however many setbacks there were. "Honestly, swimming has become my escape from reality, a bubble I don't want to come out of," she says. "I don't really know who I am without swimming." Her advice to her younger self, who constantly questioned whether she was capable of anything, would be to believe in herself. "Going to LSR was just a dream once, winning a national medal was just a dream, going to Delhi was just a dream. Now all of it is real."

For now her plans are short term: an inter college competition, qualifying through Delhi University for the All India University Games, selection for the Khelo India University Games and another Khelo India women's competition. Beyond that she has not planned much, because she does not yet know where she will do her master's or how it will affect her training. Asked to describe herself in five words, she chose stubborn, enthusiastic, self driven, independent and, she added, a social butterfly.
