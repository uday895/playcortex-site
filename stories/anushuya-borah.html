Anushuya Borah

Choosing the Water, Again and Again

"Swimming has become my escape from reality, a bubble I don't want to come out of."

Anushuya Borah's relationship with water began not as ambition but as prescription. Born with severe asthma, she was taken by her parents to Chennai as a young child, where doctors told them swimming was the only real path to healing. It helped that she was already, in her own words, a water baby, drawn to beaches and content for hours in the bathtub at home. Swimming was medicine before it was anything else.

A Fun Time, Then a Serious One

For years it stayed exactly that. She joined as part of the first batch at her local pool in Assam at age four, and school friends soon followed. There was no professional pressure in it yet, just a relaxing stretch of the afternoon. That changed three months in, when she crossed the fifty metre mark and was upgraded to the advanced group. Her coach, who also coached her mother, told them she now needed to attend regularly rather than three days a week.

What followed was a long, quiet stretch of frustration. Her health issues meant that despite being good at her level, she could not translate that into results. Six years passed without a single medal. "I lost hope and felt I wasn't meant for this," she says. It was her parents who refused to let that belief settle. In 2016 it started to turn. Selected for her first nationals, the school nationals in Delhi, she finished in the top eight of her event, and that result convinced her to take the sport seriously. That same year, her parents changed her coach, and she trained at resorts and ponds out among agricultural land, away from the attention of onlookers.

The Medal That Changed Everything

The breakthrough came in 2018, at a club competition in Sonipat, Haryana, where she won her first national gold medal. "That was the turning point," she says, "I remember thinking, now I cannot quit." From there her results kept improving: medals at nationals and state competitions, multiple group championships, and by 2020 a top 10 finish at the Khelo India Youth Games in Guwahati. But her social life had effectively ended. From sixth grade onward she barely attended school, missed birthday parties and vacations, and had, by her own account, no playtime.

Behind nearly all of it was her mother. Living in the small town of Jorhat, with a pool almost 29 kilometres from home, the family eventually bought an apartment closer to the training facility. It was her mother who travelled every day by public transport, brought her lunch, ferried her between practice and tuition, and got her home most nights around 9 or 9:30. "None of what I've achieved would have been possible without her," she says.

Covid, and the Harder Fall After

Then came Covid, and with it, the shutdown of pools. For a swimmer, even a short gap out of the water means regression. Her father built a small training area at home so she could keep working with her coach. The real difficulty came later, after she moved to Delhi for college. Without her coach, without her mother managing her diet and logistics, and without the facilities she was used to, her performance began to slip. She still made the All India University Games in her first year, but not in the shape that would have let her win a medal there.

It was not the academic workload that made things hard. It was everything surrounding it, food, travel, coaching, and above all the absence of her family. "That period in Delhi was actually tougher for me than the Covid period itself," she says, "because I had to go through it alone." The moment that crystallised it came when she had to miss the Khelo India Women's competition because her college had just reopened. Checking the medal timings later, she realised she could likely have won had she been there.

Pressure as Fuel

Where many athletes describe pressure as something to manage, Borah experiences it almost in reverse. "If there's no pressure, I can't perform well," she says. It is a mindset she pairs with a quiet pre-race ritual: standing behind the blocks, looking at the water and her lane, and visualising the race exactly as she wants it to unfold before she ever dives in.

She manages the noise of competition carefully too. Loud crowds and summer heat can overwhelm her, so she deliberately isolates before a race, usually sitting with her mother, listening to music, or talking quietly with a small circle of friends, avoiding conversation about the competition itself.

What Hasn't Changed

Outside the pool, she has a degree in contemporary and ballet dance after ten years of dancing, plays the drums, and paints. Being an athlete has cut both ways in how people see her: once dismissed by teachers as unserious about her studies, now met with the kind of pride that tends to follow visible success.

What she keeps returning to is the sport's ability to hold her, even through the periods when it stopped being fun. There was a stretch when swimming became more about competition than joy. But the joy came back, and she credits that return with why she never quit, no matter how many setbacks piled up. "I never stopped choosing swimming," she says.

For now, her focus stays close: an upcoming inter college competition, a push to qualify through Delhi University for the All India University Games, and a place in the Khelo India University Games. Asked to describe herself in five words, she settles on stubborn, enthusiastic, self driven, independent, and a social butterfly, the same girl who once needed a bathtub and a beach to feel at home, still choosing the water.
