Aditya, the Hybrid Athlete Who Learned to Just Show Up

"That taught me that if I don't show up today, the progress I'm working toward simply won't happen."

Aditya describes himself as a hybrid athlete. He is training for a half marathon on his own, has signed up for HYROX doubles, plays badminton, and has just completed a pass trek. He started running only about six months ago. His relationship with sport, though, began more than a decade earlier, with an accident.

Diwali, at ten

When he was ten, he was standing near a fire during Diwali when a spark caught his shirt. People tried to put it out with water, but it would not go out. He was taken first to Malegaon and then to Nashik for surgery. Over the next couple of years he had skin grafts taken from both thighs and placed over the burns. He wore pressure garments to manage the scarring and had a splint on his arm for around two years, at school and everywhere else. His father drove him the 12 kilometres to school and held an umbrella over the wound to keep the sun off it.

For two or three years his arm stayed fixed in one position, and without therapy the skin would have stayed tight for life. Only recently did his family tell him that doctors had warned the left side of his body might never fully recover, and that he might not be able to lift his arm. They had kept it from him on purpose. "They didn't want it to become a mental block for me. So I never grew up with that limitation in my head," he says.

He calls it a near death experience and says nothing since has come close to it as a mental battle. "During that period, my life felt like it was on pause," he says. The scars are still there, but he is in a much better place now.

Sport as recovery

After the injury his family pushed him towards sport, because his body needed it. He had played football and a little baseball before, but he settled into athletics and running, and later badminton, under a coach who had played at national level. Years of physiotherapy came alongside. It suited his nature anyway. He has always been restless, and he admits that wanting to keep doing things recently got him injured again, in Hyderabad.

Two years ago he started going to the gym. For a year and a half he saw no visible change in his physique. He kept going. "That taught me that if I don't show up today, the progress I'm working toward simply won't happen," he says. Since then he has put on muscle from around 50 to 52 kilograms last year.

Training around finance

He has just finished his degree, works in finance and is studying for the CFA. He trains in the mornings and studies in the afternoons and evenings. Deadlines make it difficult at times, but he says he has about 18 hours in a day and it is manageable.

For now that means two or three heart rate zone runs a week and the gym every day. He is vegetarian, eats roughly every four hours with two main meals, and tries to get as much protein as he can. Sundays are an easy run, because he does not believe in a completely idle day: rest is necessary, he says, but total inactivity is not. He has no coach or fixed routine yet and is building one with his HYROX partner. He takes no supplements.

Outside training he has started reading classic literature and philosophy, beginning with The Brothers Karamazov. He listens to Chris Williamson's Modern Wisdom podcast, and on Instagram he follows distance runners, HYROX and calisthenics athletes, and a creator who does everything from a wheelchair, including driving.

What comes next

His first target is the half marathon in Mumbai. After that he wants to work towards a full HYROX event, which he thinks will take around four years of training to reach a competitive level, and in the long run an ultramarathon. Once his knee and calf have recovered he plans to join a coached group at the Hindu Gymkhana in Pune, where he has trained before, and to get back to playing football.

Asked to describe himself, he chose determined, fearful, strong and peaceful. Fearful because there is always something more to improve. Strong because on one trek, when everyone else had people carrying their bags, he carried his own 10 kilogram rucksack through the snow. Peaceful because of the calm he feels when the tension releases after training.

His advice to his younger self is short. "Just show up. That's it."
