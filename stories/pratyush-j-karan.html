Pratyush J Karan on fast bowling, a broken hand and walking away

"You don't have to be so obsessed with the game. You have to go with the flow and you just have to live the sport."

Pratyush J Karan played cricket seriously for more than seven years, as a fast bowler in Delhi. About six months before this conversation, he stopped. He is 21, a student at SRCC who has chosen to do a fourth year in economics, and he is preparing for the CAT. His account of the years in between is frank about what the game gave him and what wore him down.

From the track to the crease

He did not start with cricket. In fourth standard at Sanskriti School he took up athletics, competing in the long jump and the 100 and 200 metres. He won at zonal level and at the Delhi school games and reached the state camp.

Cricket came through a close friend who was already playing seriously and went to the school team's practice sessions after hours. Pratyush joined for fun, as a batter. He was new to the side and not expected to play one match, but he was handed an over and took three wickets in it, two of them bowled. His coach decided he should be bowling. He had always loved batting, but from then on he was a fast bowler.

He rose quickly. About two months after he started, he went to the Under 14 Delhi school games trials against boys who had played for years and finished in the top 15, though as a junior he played only one match. The next year he went to the Under 17 trials, where he had to come through two rounds and compete with five bowlers, three of them already playing state cricket. He had been playing for less than a year, and he was picked. He bowled to Ranji Trophy players and current India players, and a coach he describes as one of the biggest in the country told him he had the potential of a fast bowler who could make it big. "That made me realise that I might have a shot in this game," he says.

A year and a half out

In 2019, when he was in ninth standard, he fractured his left hand just before the Under 17 season. The lockdown followed, and he could neither see a physiotherapist nor work out at home. The recovery took a year and a half. "For a fast bowler having a break of like 1.5 years is a lot in cricket," he says. He felt confused and lost, and thought about dropping the sport altogether.

He came back in 2021, in Class 11, at what he calls the best academy in Delhi, where most of the squad were already playing at a higher level and bowlers were reaching 130 to 140 kmph. He was out of rhythm and out of condition. "Every time I used to go to the ground I felt hopeless," he says. But being around better players pushed him. He practised twice a day, before and after school, started lifting weights and changed his bowling action, and after about six months he felt he was back.

His mother carried much of the load. She picked him up from school and took him straight to the academy, and when he was frustrated and missing out on trials, she helped him focus on the work rather than the outcome. Sport ran in the family. His father had also studied at SRCC and played basketball at India level, and it had been his father's dream that Pratyush would play cricket professionally.

His mother also found him a strength and conditioning coach, the man then working with the Delhi Under 19 side. He started with the fundamentals: running, endurance, a weak hamstring and the biomechanics of fast bowling. He planned Pratyush's gym sessions around his practice and trials, along with his nutrition and recovery, and a nutritionist drew up his diet. Some lessons came the hard way. Putting on weight and training heavily made him feel stronger, but a fast bowler's front foot takes about seven times his body weight, and he developed a shin injury. "Sometimes being lean also works for you," he says.

SRCC and the senior circuit

At SRCC he attended classes until 2:30 and then rushed to practice, knowing he could not match classmates who studied late into the evening and chased case competitions and placements. People kept telling him he had a good college and could leave cricket behind. He ignored them. "I wanted to prove myself," he says. Some of his coaches, for their part, told him to commit fully to one or the other. He chose to do both, and admits he did not do especially well in either.

The injury had already changed how he saw the game. Before it he was so consumed by cricket that he gave his friends little time. During the lockdown he reconnected with them, and he came to believe that obsession leaves you drained and numb when results do not come. "You don't have to be so obsessed with the game," he says. "You have to go with the flow and you just have to live the sport."

The senior circuit was harder in another way. He kept getting to trials but not past them, and he says he lacked the connections that selection in Delhi cricket can depend on. He has seen players take more wickets than anyone and still miss out. His mindset shifted too. As a younger player he thought only about his training and bowling in the present. Later he compared himself with others, counted the wickets he needed for selection and overthought his performance, his routines and his gym work. Frustration wore away his hunger for the game. He believes the younger version of himself had the better approach.

A different target

Six months after stepping away, he says it has been very hard but he has managed. He has moved his cricket energy into academics: internships to build a corporate CV, an extra year at SRCC, and the CAT, with his sights on the top business schools. His routines have barely changed. He still plans his days and still trains, but the goal is different.

If money were no object, he would spend it first on his health, on a good gym and diet, and on his mental health, including breaks and trips so that he does not burn out. His advice to his younger self is simple: do not overcomplicate things, think about what you can do in the present rather than the outcome, and set realistic goals.
