Pratyush J Karan

A fast bowler's honest second innings

"You don't have to over complicate stuff. You have to think about what you could do in the present right now."

Pratyush J Karan played cricket professionally for more than seven years. Then, about six months before this conversation, the SRCC student made one of the hardest decisions an athlete can make and stepped away. His story is not a tale of failure. It is an unusually candid account of what competitive sport can give a young person, what it can take, and how the energy it builds can be pointed somewhere new.

From the sprint track to the crease

His sporting life began in athletics in the fourth standard, with long jump and 100 and 200 metre sprints, good enough to win zonal and Delhi school events and reach the state camp. Cricket arrived almost by accident. He joined his school's after-hours practice for fun, as a batsman. In one match he was handed the ball and took three wickets, breaking the stumps twice in a single over. His coach saw something, and Pratyush switched to fast bowling for good.

The rise was startling. Within about two months of playing seriously, he made the top 15 in under-14 school games trials, competing against boys who had trained for years. In under-17 trials he outperformed bowlers who were already playing state cricket. He bowled to Ranji and international players, and a highly respected coach told him he had the potential to make it big.

The fracture and the lost year and a half

Then, in 2019, at what felt like the peak of his early career, he fractured his left hand just before the season. The COVID lockdown made consistent physiotherapy impossible, and the recovery stretched to a year and a half. For a fast bowler, that is an eternity. He describes feeling genuinely lost and thinking about dropping the sport altogether.

When he returned in 2021, in Class 11, he joined a top Delhi academy where most of the squad were state players, many of them bowling at 130 to 140 kmph while he was still finding his rhythm. He felt hopeless walking onto the ground. But surrounding himself with better players also motivated him. He practised twice a day, before and after school, started lifting weights, and after six months he was back.

Mother, method and the body

Pratyush credits his mother for carrying him through those years, picking him up from school and taking him straight to the academy, and helping him focus on long-term outcomes rather than each setback. His father, also an SRCC alumnus, had played basketball at India level, and sport was always part of the family. Working with a nutritionist and the Delhi Under-19 strength and conditioning coach, who planned his gym sessions around his trials and practice, he learned to take fitness seriously. He also learned that bulking up is not always right for a fast bowler, whose front foot absorbs several times his body weight, after a shin injury taught him that leaner sometimes worked better.

Obsession, burnout and balance

At SRCC, he rushed to practice the moment classes ended at 2:30, knowing he could not match classmates who studied late into the evening. Looking back, he admits he was so obsessed with cricket that he neglected friendships and hobbies. The injury taught him that extreme obsession can lead to burnout and numbness when results do not come. He learned to live the sport rather than be consumed by it.

Moving into the senior circuit, he found himself overthinking outcomes, counting the wickets he needed for selection and comparing himself with others. Frustration with a crowded and often opaque selection system wore down his hunger. He says his younger mindset, focused only on the present and the joy of bowling, was the better one.

Pointing the energy somewhere new

Today Pratyush has shifted his cricket energy into academics. He is preparing for the CAT, aiming for top business schools, has started internships to build a corporate CV, and is completing a fourth year at SRCC. If he had infinite money, he would invest first in health, good nutrition and mental well-being, because he knows how quickly burnout arrives. His advice to his younger self, and to any young athlete, is the hard-won lesson of his whole journey: set realistic goals, flow with the process, and focus on what you can do right now.
