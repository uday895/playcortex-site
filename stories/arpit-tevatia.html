Arpit Tevatia

Arpit Tevatia, the midfielder who learnt his football against grown men

"Some things are never meant to be for you, so you should accept it."

Arpit Tevatia was 14 or 15 when he started playing for the Delhi Police senior team, a teenage midfielder holding his own against grown men. "That's the point where I improved a lot," he says. He went on to play for several Delhi clubs and for Shri Ram College of Commerce. For now he has stepped back from football to focus on his studies and on finding a job, though he says he is trying to get back on track as soon as he can.

From KV Shalimar Bagh to Delhi Police

He started in the sixth or seventh standard at Kendriya Vidyalaya Shalimar Bagh, playing with friends until a coach invited him to a trial for the regional team. He was selected, and that was his first proper match. Because his family has a police background, he was eligible for a programme the Family Welfare Society started around 2018 or 2019 to support young athletes. A friend told him to try out. He had lower back pain during the trial but gave everything he had and made the team. His game improved day by day, and then came the Delhi Police senior side.

He pulled his friends along with him. One of them was a goalkeeper, and Arpit would drag him out of his house so he could practise his shooting.

For Classes 11 and 12 he moved to Modern School, the school Sunil Chhetri attended. There he played zonals and interzonals and gave trials for national teams. He calls it the peak of his career.

Getting into SRCC

As a state champion, he held a certificate that made him eligible for university admission through the sports quota. The day before his entrance exam he was out on the ground playing, sure his football would carry him. On his preference list he first put PGDAV and other colleges where he could simply play. Before the final selection, seniors and the SRCC coach told him to consider SRCC, so he moved it to the top. In 2023 he was admitted to study economics honours. "Getting into SRCC is totally something that I never dreamed of," he says.

At SRCC he was in the starting eleven throughout. In his first year he and two friends, Amar and Harish, were the only first years in a team otherwise made up of second and third year students. He played through injuries and never missed a crucial match, while keeping up with attendance and internal assessments, which he says were the real pressure. Being in the college football team, he says, was a proud thing.

The back injury

His biggest setback came at 17 or 18, at a national camp where he was competing with Under 23 players. He hurt his lower back so badly that while chasing the ball he had to stop and walk with a hand on his back. It happened during his final selection dates, when he was in the eleven and had the chance to represent Delhi. "I even cried on that," he says. "I blamed God a lot. But some things are never meant to be for you, so you should accept it."

At SRCC a second problem arrived: a muscle pain around his knee that appears when he bends deeply, for example in squats. He has seen several physios and had an MRI, but nothing serious has shown up and no one has found the cause. It has taken something away from his shooting and his change of direction.

He thinks his Class 11 routine played a part in his injuries. He lived in Model Town and the school was in West Delhi, about two hours away by bus, so he woke at five. In summer, training ran from 2 pm until 4 pm at full intensity, with long spells of running and no breaks. Later, when training moved to the mornings, he would train, go to classes, travel home, go to the gym and then play again in the evening, with little food and no guidance. "I don't know what I was doing," he says. He also remembers it as fun: walking to the bus stop with friends, making fun of each other, no study pressure in Class 11.

Belief without ego

A Manchester United fan with a Cristiano Ronaldo wallpaper on his phone, Arpit admires Ronaldo's mentality and discipline. After a setback he tells himself, "I am good, I can do it, I can easily do everything, I'm the best." He keeps that separate from ego. After trials he goes to the coaches to ask what he can improve, and if he is wrong he accepts it and works on it. Outside sport he describes himself as a learner who wants to learn things at the highest level. "I don't settle for less," he says.

Early on, pressure showed up as anxiety at trials. At Under 15 national trials, he remembers, players could be made to sit for a whole day waiting for their chance. Experience and match time took that away, and he now rarely feels pressure stepping onto the field. What stays with him is his own performance. If he plays badly he blames himself and spends the night going over it, imagining scenarios.

His parents, he says, have always been "chill". They have never interfered and have let him make his own decisions. "I'm very lucky that I never got any pressure from my family."

What comes next

He managed his own nutrition. He was fully vegetarian until someone suggested eggs, and he later added whey protein to a basic Indian diet, with coffee and a banana before training. He believes players at Under 15 and Under 18 level need a professional nutritionist, because they are still growing and need guidance on diet and recovery.

Arpit is now doing internships while he looks for a full time role. His short term plan is to get back into good form. Further ahead he wants a stable job, fitness that is up to the mark, the CFA alongside work and the CAT after some experience. Above all, he says, he wants "to enjoy life".
