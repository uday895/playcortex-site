Aditi Gulati

Aditi Gulati's long road from a Gurgaon court to college tennis in New York

"Let's be honest, I'm not some gifted Carlos Alcaraz. Everything that I am right now is just because of my hard work and my family's hard work."

Aditi Gulati is quick to say she is not a natural. "Let's be honest, I'm not some gifted Carlos Alcaraz," she says. "Everything that I am right now is just because of my hard work and my family's hard work." Born in 2007, she grew up first in Gurgaon and then in Dubai, and has spent most of her life on tennis courts. She is now trying to build a professional career while studying in the United States.

A racket she did not want

She was five, living with her family in a small complex in Gurgaon that had a tennis court, when her father handed her a tennis racket, a golf club and a football. She, her twin brother and her older brother played all three. By her father's account, she hated tennis. He told the coach who worked in the complex that he would not be paid for the month if Aditi skipped class. She skipped once, and for a while afterwards the coach would ring the doorbell and take her to practice himself.

What changed her mind was a tournament at a school in Gurgaon where she finished runner up. At seven, her father took the three children downstairs and told them to pick one sport. Her brothers chose golf. She chose tennis, "trying to be different", she thinks, and soon she was asking her coach to come down at odd hours, playing at six in the morning and for four or five hours a day.

Growing up in Dubai

When the family moved to Dubai, at around the age of nine, they found her first coach by asking the building's security guard. He knew Ron Manuja, who had played Davis Cup for India and ran an academy, and with him she began to take the game seriously. She later moved to a bigger academy and played the under 10, under 12, under 14 and under 18 categories at the same time, with her mother sitting through whole weekends of matches. In a junior masters series whose winners earned a fully paid trip to Paris to watch Roland Garros, she believes she was the first player to qualify in all four categories. Over two years she won the under 10 and under 12 events.

Her parents were never the kind to watch training. From the age of ten she took the Dubai metro to tennis on her own, and after a year or two her mother stopped coming to tournaments too. The family had one fixed rule: everyone was home for dinner at 8.30. In her last years of school, when the heat made afternoon sessions impossible, she would eat at six, sit at the table with the family at 8.30 and then train from nine until midnight before school the next morning.

A UAE Ministry of Education programme for students who train outside school allowed her and her brother, among its first students in grade seven, to miss lessons when they needed to and catch up afterwards. She still missed the overnight school camps, because Friday nights led into Saturdays and Sundays, "prime tournament time".

Going international

When COVID arrived she moved to the first academy that reopened. The coach there trained his players almost as if for the military. For about three weeks they were not allowed to touch a racket, and in the first week they ran from three in the afternoon until eight with no break. It built her stamina, but in time she felt she had outgrown his style. "I don't really want to play tennis while being yelled at by the coach across the net," she says.

At 14, in 2021, she played her first international event, in Ghana, on a family trip that summer. A few months later, in Togo, she earned her first ITF points. Between 2021 and 2024 she played the junior circuit, travelling mostly with her father, reaching a semi final in Kuwait and winning an ITF junior doubles title in Fujairah and an ITF title in the UAE. For a long time she was the UAE's number two in women's tennis, trading the top two places with one other player.

The Kuwait semi final taught her something about herself. She won the first set in about 23 minutes. Midway through the second, her father, the only person there cheering for her, got up and left. She lost her concentration, then her lead, then the set. "I didn't realise that I had that much connection to my dad watching," she says.

She moved through several academies before settling at Top Spin with a Serbian coach, Milan, who saw her through her ITF titles and her deep runs in tournaments. More recently she has trained with Vera Zvonareva, the former WTA world number two. The coach she credits most, though, is John Guntley. "He's really the one who made me love tennis here," she says. In an academy system where she says it is easy to get lost, he saw her want and passion for the game. His wife, a nutritionist, also looked over the weekly food lists Aditi wrote out.

The humbling road to college

Her mother is a school principal and her father a lawyer, and university was never optional. She could go professional, they said, but alongside a degree. Her father, a former professional squash player who looked after the sporting side and travelled with her, was just as clear that she should not give up the game after so much work. Her twin brother went through the same process in golf and now plays Division 1 golf at the University of Southern California.

Recruitment started late, in 2023, after her Grade 10 board exams. From 2024 injuries and illness kept her out of tournaments, so her ranking fell, and US coaches rely on the Universal Tennis Rating, which was hard to raise in Dubai, where UTR events were few and the level low. Coaches who saw her play were keen. Those who only saw her numbers were not. At ID camps at Lehigh and Harvard in 2023 she received a Division 1 offer from George Washington University, and later a full scholarship offer from UMass Amherst. She calls the whole process humbling. "Suddenly you're not just competing against people in Dubai," she says, and an international player "sitting 8,000 miles away" has to rely mostly on videos.

Admissions brought a series of twists. Pomona College, then second in Division 3, turned her down at the athlete pre read stage despite a predicted 45 out of 45 in the IB Diploma. Columbia, where she had met the coach in December 2024, deferred and then rejected her early decision application. She applied to Pomona again through regular decision, was admitted, and the coach, who had been trying to recruit her for months, welcomed her to the team. She played a semester there, including at nationals, and valued the structure of college sport, with lifting, practice, mobility and recovery all planned for her. But she wanted Division 1 tennis to give herself a better chance of turning professional. She applied to transfer to Columbia, was admitted academically, and in the summer of 2026 was finalising her place on the team before moving to New York in September.

Fighting her own body

Injuries have shaped the last few years more than any opponent. She lists shoulder tendinitis three or four times, problems in both knees, torn ligaments and several ankle injuries. Most recently came stress fractures in both legs, with torn fascia. Doctors in the US told her she would certainly need surgery. Back in Dubai, a doctor identified the bone problem and set out to avoid an operation, using injections, neuro resonance therapy and about 16 physio sessions. The day before she spoke about her career, she had played tennis for the first time in two months.

At its worst, she says, the question was no longer whether she could play but whether she would walk properly, and her parents were clear that tennis was not worth that. "I think the worst thing you can do is be negative," she says. "Much, much easier said than done." Her mother's positivity helped. On some days she simply walked down to the court, bounced a ball and came back, to keep the connection. "I've genuinely not missed doing something ever so much in my life."

Bollywood between points

Her biggest problem on court is overthinking. When she is nervous she gets jumpy, rushes her finish and lifts her head to see where the ball has gone before she has properly hit it. So she reminds herself to watch the ball at the contact point. Between points she walks back to the fence and hums whatever song is stuck in her head, usually an upbeat Hindi Bollywood number. "If I tell myself to focus more, then I'm just putting more pressure on myself," she says. At Pomona she added Hindi songs to the team's practice playlist, and before she left, her teammates asked her to play her music at a last session together at night.

College tennis brought a new kind of pressure. Three doubles matches count for one point and six singles for one each, so a dual match can come down to a single court with the whole team depending on it. She also thinks of tennis as the loneliest sport, because professionals cannot speak to anyone during a match. For a few years she worked with an online sports psychology group, Simikra Sports, until her psychologist left. One exercise she was given, a "13 plus" game, seemed silly to her at first. She later found it very useful.

Beyond the court

At school she played almost everything, from basketball and athletics to opening the batting in cricket, and she performed kathak. She is also full of ideas. She is building an app for college students with a cofounder and researching a real time tennis coaching app, and her brother teases her about the new venture she pitches to him almost every day.

Her parents still plan ahead. Her mother recently printed out a five year plan, and they insist on a realistic Plan B, because one injury can end a career. Aditi's tennis goals are to raise her UTR and earn a professional ranking, starting with ITF events such as W15s and W35s and the UTR Pro Tennis Tour in the US. If tennis does not work out, she studies economics and politics and would aim for management consulting and, in the long run, "a startup that sticks". For now her nearest goal is simpler. "I just want to enjoy striking the ball," she says.
