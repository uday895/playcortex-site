Abir Srivastava

Grief, grit and the gift of playing

"It's not the gift of being a good footballer, a great footballer. It's the gift of being able to play football."

For Abir Srivastava, sport has never been one thing. It has been cricket in the gully, football in boarding school, javelin and sprints at the All India University Games, and hockey for good measure. The 22-year-old law student at Jindal Global Law School has played something every day for as long as he can remember. Two ACL injuries tried to take that away. Instead, they changed how he sees the game, and himself.

Cricket first, then Messi

Cricket was his first love. With his father posted in Visakhapatnam with the Navy, Abir would return to Delhi for holidays and take cricket coaching near home, and he still remembers getting his first SG bat. Football, by contrast, he disliked, mostly because everyone around him was obsessed with it. That changed during the 2014 World Cup, when his father sat him down and told him to watch the world sport. Abir backed Brazil, until he saw Lionel Messi score a long-range strike against Iran. Within minutes, he says, he was an Argentina fan, and soon he had a pair of blue and white Argentina boots.

Mayo, captaincy and Rajasthan

In seventh grade, at Mayo College in Ajmer, he went to a cricket session that was not running properly and decided to try football with a friend instead. Years of playing with older boys paid off. Within a year he was captain of the under-14 team, winning at district level and going on to represent Rajasthan at state level. The passion of boarding school football, playing in front of classmates against fierce rivals, taught him that football is about representing a group of people who are there to cheer you on. COVID then took away much of his football in Classes 11 and 12.

At college he made a promise to himself to make sport central again, joining the football, athletics and hockey teams and travelling to the All India University Games in Odisha for javelin and the 100 metres.

Two knees, one long road back

Then came the injuries. First a partial ACL tear with meniscus damage, repaired surgically. Six months later, his knee gave way on the field and he suffered a complete ACL tear, needing a second surgery and around 10 months out. For someone who had played three hours a day since the age of five, he calls it one of the most distressing periods of his life.

The physical recovery, he says, was the easy part. The psychological battle was harder: not knowing whether you will play again, after being so disciplined, and suddenly having everything stop. He went through denial and anger before acceptance. He learned that it takes a lot of patience to be patient, that there is no shame in a wheelchair or a walker, and that you can ask for help. Inspired by Rishabh Pant's words about how much we try to plan our lives, he learned to take things as they come. Next to his surgery scar, he now has a tattoo: Grief, Grit, Glory.

Parents, peers and opportunity cost

His parents have always told him to do whatever he chooses, but to do it well. His father did not fully understand the injury at first, but watching Abir's rehab changed that, and he now says he is grateful sport has kept his son away from the unwanted parts of life. Most of Abir's closest friends are people he has shared a field with, who understand that sport is sometimes simply about being somewhere you feel at home.

He speaks about opportunity cost with real clarity. Chasing a big gym physique made him slower and put more load on his knees, so he shifted to sport-specific training, plyometrics and agility, with lower body work twice a week to protect his knees. He skips the partying lifestyle because it harms recovery. He tracks his nutrition himself, mastering the basics: calories, high protein and consistency.

Not a quitter

People told him two surgeries were enough. Abir knew the risks, and went back anyway. He does not see himself as a quitter, and he believes every athlete has that resilience. Now entering his final year of law school, his goals are a good job placement, as much sport as possible before college ends, and continued physical and mental well-being. He wants to be happy and at peace, because that, he believes, is when athletes perform at their best.
