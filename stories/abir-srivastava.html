Abir Srivastava

Abir Srivastava and the gift of being able to play

"It takes a lot of patience to just be patient for some time."

Abir Srivastava has a tattoo of Lionel Messi on his leg, a dog named after him, and still keeps his first pair of football boots. Beside the surgery scar on his knee is a second tattoo, which reads "Grief, Grit, Glory". Between them, the two tattoos tell much of his story: a boy who came to football late and reluctantly, fell for it completely, and then had to work out what the game meant to him when two knee injuries took it away.

He is 22 and entering his final year at Jindal Global Law School in Sonipat. He plays football above all, but for as long as he can remember he has played whatever was in front of him. "If I saw a cricket bat somewhere, I picked up a cricket bat," he says. "Sport for forever has been my identity, has been my source of joy and happiness."

Cricket first, then Messi

Cricket came first. His father was in the Navy and posted in Visakhapatnam, and when the family came back to Delhi for the holidays, he took Abir to cricket coaching near their home. Abir was five or six. He remembers his first SG bat, bought at a discount from a sports shop run by a friend of his mother's, and endless games in the park and below the house.

Football he actively disliked. It had suddenly become popular, and his cousins and friends wanted to play and watch nothing else, so he dug in on the other side. His father, a huge football fan, kept telling him he did not have to play but should at least watch the world's sport. During the 2014 World Cup, when Abir was 10, he finally sat down with him. He backed Brazil, the hosts and favourites. Then his father told him to watch Argentina against Iran, and he saw Messi score a long range shot into the top corner. "I started off as a Brazil fan but five minutes into watching that first Argentina game my life changed," he says. A week later his father came home with a pair of blue and white boots with Argentina written on them.

His father has recently retired from the Navy, and this summer the two of them watched every match of the World Cup together.

Mayo College and Rajasthan

In seventh grade he went to boarding school at Mayo College in Ajmer. New boys spent their first month trying different sports, and Abir went to cricket. A visiting coach was running a camp for the senior players, so the juniors were not getting proper sessions. He had just got his new boots, so he went along to football with a friend who was already in the team. Years of playing with older boys had made him better than he expected. Within a year he was captain of the school's Under 14 side. They won the district tournament, and he was selected at state level.

He then moved up to the Under 17 team, spending much of the first year on the bench. He and a friend, Adhi, were picked for the school side early on, and both went on to be selected for Rajasthan. The school's own competition, the Mayo Football Tournament, brought in rival schools from across the region, and the atmosphere of those games stayed with him. "Football is more than just sport," he says. "You're representing a particular group of people and they are here to cheer you on."

Covid arrived just as he entered Class 11, which should have been his best years of school football. He finished school in 2022 having played very little, and spent those two years doing debate and Model UN online instead. He made himself a promise that in college he would play as much sport as he possibly could.

Two knee injuries

He kept that promise. At Jindal he plays football and hockey and is on the athletics team, which he briefly captained. He has sat on the sports council for a few years and is due to take over as its president next semester. He went to the All India University Games at KIIT in Odisha with the athletics team, running the 100 metres and throwing the javelin.

Then came the injuries. He first tore his ACL partially and damaged his meniscus, which was repaired without a full reconstruction. Six months later his knee gave way on the field and the ACL tore completely. He needed a second surgery and was out for around ten months. For someone who had played three hours a day since he was five, he calls it one of the most distressing periods of his life.

The physical work, he says, was the easier part, because athletes have trained all their lives. The harder part was not knowing whether he would play again at all. "The first question one tends to ask is why me," he says. He had been disciplined and had sat out things other people were doing, and then everything stopped. Some of his closest friends were in their final year, playing what should have been their last tournaments together, and he could only be there with them from his wheelchair.

He went through denial and anger before he reached acceptance. For a month and a half he could not walk. He learned that there was no shame in using a wheelchair or a walker, and that it is fine to ask for help. "It takes a lot of patience to just be patient for some time," he says. His favourite quote comes from Rishabh Pant, who was asked after his car accident what he had learned. Pant's answer, as Abir remembers it, was simply that we tend to plan a lot. It taught Abir to take things as they come.

His father had always joked that Abir's generation could not work hard. His father had been a swimmer, had never seen an injury on the field, and at first did not fully grasp how serious it was, though his mother did. After watching a year and a half of rehabilitation and physiotherapy, and speaking to the doctors, his father sat Abir down and told him he had been wrong about him.

The gift of football

Abir chose law rather than a professional career in sport, partly because of the injuries. He went back to football anyway. Plenty of people told him that two surgeries were enough and that his knee might not survive a third injury. "I don't think I'm a quitter," he says. "I knew the risks." He drew strength from seniors who had come back from injuries of their own, and he believes resilience is something every athlete shares rather than something special to him.

What changed most was how he plays. "I'm playing more football now than I have in my entire life because I don't take it that seriously anymore," he says. He no longer thinks of his gift as being a great footballer. "It's the gift of being able to play football." A six a side game with friends or an intense eleven a side match is enough to make his day. The tattoo beside his scar carries the same idea: there will be moments of grief, he explains, but with grit, resilience and passion, glory will follow.

Training for the long run

When he left school he wanted to get big. He started lifting, taking protein and pre workout, doing everything a teenager sees on Instagram. The more muscle he put on, the slower he became on the field, and the more load went through his knees. That, he believes, was when the trouble with his knee began. "There's always an opportunity cost when it comes to sports," he says. He moved his training towards plyometrics, agility and work specific to football, and decided a bigger physique could wait until he is 26 or 27.

He now goes to the gym three or four times a week, with two lower body sessions to protect his knees and one or two for the upper body. Mostly he tries to play, because game time sharpens his skills and instincts and keeps him fit. The same thinking keeps him away from a partying lifestyle, which he sees as bad for recovery and performance. He tracks his own nutrition on MyFitnessPal and sticks to the basics: know your calories, keep protein high, eat enough carbohydrate for energy and stay consistent. He also admits to cheating on his diet now and then, because he lives to eat.

Final year

Law school is demanding, and he says it took him time to remember that a student athlete is also a student. In his final year, getting a job placement comes first. He also wants to play as much as he can before college ends, knowing that regular sport will be harder to fit in afterwards. His two main goals are to build his career and to keep improving his fitness and wellbeing, mentally as well as physically. "If I'm not doing some kind of fitness here and there, being part of some activity, I just can't function," he says.
