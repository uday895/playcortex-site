Shrey Sarin

Shrey Sarin, the pistol shooter who chose law

"I would rather work for 14 hours a day in an office than do 3 hours worth of shooting in terms of mental exhaustion."

In December 2025, Shrey Sarin had not picked up a pistol for a year. He went to the nationals almost for the fun of it, and for the first ten minutes his name sat at the top of the leaderboard, above shooters who had given their lives to the sport. He only found out when his brother, who had been watching from the spectators' stand, showed him a photo of the scores. All that time, Shrey had been thinking about his dog, who was unwell, and about a party he was going to that night.

That is how shooting has always worked for him. It is, he says, roughly 30 per cent physical and 70 per cent mental, and the less he thinks about the match, the better he shoots. Shrey is 20, has just graduated from St. Stephen's College and is about to start law school. He has stopped serious training, but he still shoots at state and national level and is ranked 11th in the country in the 50 metre event.

A family roadmap

He started in July 2017, aged 11, at the Siri Fort range in Delhi. His father and his brother, who is four and a half years older, handed him an air pistol and told him he might like it. His father had loved guns and wanted to shoot as a boy, but Shrey's grandfather steered him towards his studies and the law, the family profession for four generations. He passed that passion on to his elder son, and Shrey followed them both. "My dad does this, I also want to be like my dad," he says.

Shooting suited him for another reason. He was a big child who loved football and was, by his own account, terrible at it. "I don't think there's anyone in this world who's worse at football than I am," he says. Shooting was a sport he could play without running, and having grown up on video games, he liked the idea of it. He got good quickly, shot his first nationals at 12 in 2018, and by 15 had won three Delhi state gold medals. He was always the youngest at the range and would talk the older shooters into shot by shot competitions.

His first big win came at the 2018 Delhi state championship: a team gold, shared with his brother and one other shooter. His grandfather was the chief guest and put the medal around his neck. The next morning at Vasant Valley School, his principal presented it to him again at assembly. "It was like a high," he says. "I just loved it because I wanted to win." He admits to a competitive streak that extends to brushing his teeth faster than anyone else.

Seventy per cent mental

At 10 metres, the ten ring is tiny, and the gap between a ten and a nine is a matter of millimetres. Even one coke or one coffee can make the hand shake. So as a teenager he gave up sugar for a year and went three years without coffee or cola. Shooting barely moves the body, so he did a lot of cardio on the treadmill and left arm only dumbbell work to balance the right arm that holds the pistol. His coach kept strength training light, worried that building muscle would stiffen his shoulders and back. He was also made to do yoga, meditation and journaling. "I hated it so much," he says. Looking back, he is glad he did it. The other lesson was to choose one person, whether his coach, his father or his brother, and follow their advice completely.

The hardest work was in his head. A 10 metre match lasts about an hour and a half, in a hall with around 80 other shooters and the same 1980s and 1990s playlist of Cyndi Lauper and Michael Jackson playing to cover the sound of other shots. Every shooter is alone. "Shooting is one v one v one with 800 other people," he says. When a shot goes wrong there is no one else to blame, and an experienced shooter usually knows exactly what went wrong, which makes it worse. At 15 and 16 he was very hard on himself after a single bad shot, while older shooters kept telling him to let it go and carry on.

He calls it a fun sport but also the most draining one he knows. "I would rather work for 14 hours a day in an office than do 3 hours worth of shooting in terms of mental exhaustion," he says. The aim is to repeat exactly the same action perfectly, 60 times in a row, and no one has ever shot 600 out of 600.

It also humbled him. He describes walking into the range as the most overconfident kid there, sure he was better than everyone as his father's son and his brother's brother, and then being humbled by his first poor shot. He thinks he would have been unbearable to be around if shooting had not done that. He still believes a little ego helps a shooter, as long as it never becomes delusion. He has seen shooters post championship winning scores in training and fall apart in matches for lack of belief. He has also seen how rivals use the silence. One, his closest competitor at the time, finished his own match, sat directly behind Shrey with his feet up and ate his food, knowing it would unsettle him. Shrey admits it may have played a small part in his poor result that day.

The sick day

The match he remembers most came in December 2022, during his Class 12 pre board exams. He was due to shoot his last CBSE competition, at a range north of Punjabi Bagh, and woke up vomiting. He rang his brother to say he was not going. Then the organiser called to say he had been moved to the afternoon detail and should come and shoot whatever he could. He went in his chappals with his shooting shoes in his bag, sweating and pale, and spent the match complaining to himself about how ill he felt. His series scores climbed as the match went on. He shot 384 or 385 out of 400, beat the entire field, and then went to the bathroom and was sick. "It was like an otherworldly experience," he says. No competition, before or since, has brought him as much praise.

Choosing law

His brother shaped the biggest decision too. His brother loved shooting even more than Shrey, went to the world championships twice and won a silver once, and then gave it up for law school. He is now a litigator. Watching that, Shrey decided he could not reach the Indian team, give everything to the sport and then have to let it go. Shooting at that level is a full time job, with national camps, constant travel and long days at the range. "There's no balance," he says. His father had offered him the chance to make shooting his main path and fit his education around it, and most people around him told him to keep going for as long as he could. He chose law.

"It was a choice that I made and I can't tell if I'm happy with it or not happy with it," he says. "But I miss it more than everything."

Shooting helped him into St. Stephen's through the sports quota, which there comes down to a single seat. He is frank about how much the sport costs: an air pistol runs into lakhs of rupees, firearms several lakhs more, and pellets and ammunition have to be bought constantly unless you are in the Indian team. For two years he received two lakh rupees a year in government support for his sporting expenses. His advice to curious children is to start at a public range such as Siri Fort, pay the fee and see whether they love it.

Still part of the range

Shrey never let shooting become his whole personality. He listened to jazz and classical music, Beethoven and Mozart, to calm himself during competitions, and he loves cars and football, supporting Manchester United like his father. He calls himself genuinely family oriented. "My life revolves around my family," he says. He wants to be a litigator, then a senior advocate and perhaps a judge. "Maybe Chief Justice of India. You never know." In the meantime he still shoots with his brother, still turns up for state and national competitions, and hopes to remain part of the shooting community.
