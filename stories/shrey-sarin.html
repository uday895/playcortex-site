Shrey Sarin

Thirteen seconds of perfect stillness

"You don't have to make things that you're good about be your personality. You can let your personality be your personality."

Shooting, Shrey Sarin says, is roughly 30 percent physical and 70 percent mental. It is a sport where you stand still for an hour and a half, compete one against hundreds in near silence, and have no one to blame but yourself when a shot drifts. For more than a decade, the Delhi pistol shooter thrived in it. Now, as he heads to law school, he has made peace with stepping back from the professional level, without ever stepping away from the range.

Following his father and brother

Shrey grew up with shooting in the family. His father is an international-level shooter and a world champion, and his brother, four and a half years older, took it up first. At 11, in July 2017, Shrey followed them to the Siri Fort range in Delhi. He admits the early appeal was partly that it was a sport he could do as a big kid who loved football but was not much good at it. He also grew up on video games and was fascinated by the precision of the sport.

He got good very quickly, shot his first nationals at around 12, and won his first big medal the same year: a team gold at the 2018 Delhi state championship. The moment his principal put the medal around his neck in front of the school assembly, he says, he fell in love with it. By 15 he had won three Delhi state golds, and his training peaked in Classes 11 and 12, three to four times a week.

Sacrifice and patience

At 10 metres, the scoring ring is tiny, and even one coke or one coffee can make your hands shake. So, as a teenager, Shrey cut out sugar and caffeine for years. He had to do cardio and left-arm-only dumbbell work to balance a body that barely moves while shooting. He did yoga, meditation and journaling, which he resisted at 12 or 13 but is grateful for now. The biggest lesson, he says, was to follow one coach's advice completely.

Shooting also gave a very impatient person somewhere to escape. You are in your own world, alone with yourself for two hours. It taught him patience, and slowly, to be less hard on himself after one bad shot. He arrived at the range as a young teenager feeling like the best, and the first poor shot humbled him quickly. A little ego helps, he believes, as long as it never tips into delusion.

The sick day and the ten minutes

Two days stand out. In December 2022, during pre-board exams, he was violently ill on the morning of his final CBSE competition. He went anyway, sweating and nauseous, and shot one of the best matches of his life, a 384 out of 400, beating everyone. And in December 2025, after nearly a year away from training, he sat at the top of the nationals leaderboard for the first ten minutes. His secret both times was to think about anything but the match, including his dog and a party that night.

Choosing law

Watching his brother give up serious shooting for law school became Shrey's roadmap. Competing at the elite level, he explains, is a full-time job, with national camps, constant travel and long days at the range. He decided he could not give that to a sport that is 70 percent mental and still give his best to a demanding academic path. His shooting achievements helped him earn admission to St. Stephen's College through the sports quota, and he has just graduated. It was his choice, and he is thankful for the time it has given him, even though he misses shooting a lot.

He is also candid about the cost of the sport. Equipment runs into lakhs of rupees and ammunition is expensive, which is why he urges interested kids to start at public facilities like Siri Fort to see if they love it.

Family first, shooting always

Shrey never let shooting become his whole personality, and he thinks that is a good thing. He listens to jazz and classical music to calm his mind, and loves cars and football. He calls himself genuinely family-oriented, and his professional goal is ambitious: to become a litigator, a senior advocate and, one day perhaps, a judge. He still shoots with his brother and wants to stay part of the shooting community. For a sport that taught him stillness, it seems fitting that it remains a steady part of his life.
